1. Approach and scope

This tool implements the approximation in Choi, Liu and Liu (2025), which builds on Merton (1969). The structure has two layers.

Layer one gives the share of total wealth that belongs in equities. This is the Merton share, covered in sections 2 and 3.

Layer two accounts for the fact that most of a working household's total wealth is the present value of wages not yet earned, which cannot be sold or borrowed against. It converts the layer one answer into an instruction about the tradeable portion. This is Bodie, Merton and Samuelson (1992), implemented through an approximation fitted to the numerical solution of Cocco, Gomes and Maenhout (2005). Sections 4 and 5.

Combined, the calculation is one expression:

wfin = clip( [ln(1+μ) − ln(1+r)] / (γ ⋅ σ2) × (1 + HC/W), 0, 1 )
(1)

where

wfin
is the share of the investable portfolio to hold in equities, the figure the tool reports
μ
is the expected annual return on the equity portfolio, after inflation
r
is the annual return on a safe asset, also after inflation
γ
is relative risk aversion, on a scale of 1 to 10
σ
is the annual volatility of the equity portfolio
HC
is human capital: the present value of future wages and Social Security benefits
W
is investable financial wealth today
clip
holds the result between 0 and 1, ruling out shorting and leverage

The first factor is the Merton share. The second, 1 + HC/W, is total wealth divided by financial wealth. The clip is a constraint imposed from outside, not a result of the model.

The source spreadsheet this tool replaces computes only the first factor. Choi's spreadsheet computes the whole expression but with simpler inputs: one equity asset, a fixed 5% expected return, no valuation input. This tool takes the input estimation from the former and the human capital treatment from the latter. It is calibrated for a household in the United States, for the reason given in section 7.2.

1.1 Who this is for

The model answers one question and it is worth naming, because a reader who brings the wrong question to it will get a confident answer to something they did not ask.

It answers: of the money a United States household has invested and does not need on any particular date, what share belongs in equities rather than in a safe bond. It assumes wages or a pension are coming in, and that the money is meant to last the rest of a life.

It does not answer anything with a date on it. A house deposit in three years, school fees in eight, a known payment on a known day: none of that fits, and the reason is structural rather than a missing feature. Section 2.3 shows that horizon does not appear in the Merton share at all, which is a result and not an omission. A model in which horizon does not appear cannot distinguish money needed in three years from money needed in forty, so it cannot be asked to. Goal-based allocation is a different problem with a different solution, and this tool has nothing to say about it.

It also does not say how much to save, which securities or funds to hold, or what to do about a house, a business, a mortgage or an annuity. Section 9 lists what is excluded and why.

2. Layer one: the Merton share

The investor has constant relative risk aversion, meaning the percentage of wealth put at risk does not change as wealth changes. Utility is

U(W) = W(1 − γ) / (1 − γ)
(2)

where

U(W)
is utility from ending with wealth W
W
is final wealth
γ
is relative risk aversion, controlling how sharply utility flattens as wealth rises

The flattening is what produces risk aversion: each additional dollar adds less utility than the one before, so a gain does not offset an equally likely loss of the same size. Higher γ means faster flattening.

2.1 The trade-off

Place a fraction w of wealth in equities and 1 − w in the safe asset. Over a short period, maximising expected utility is equivalent to maximising the certainty equivalent, the guaranteed return the investor would accept in place of the uncertain one. To second order:

CE(w) = r + w(μ − r) − (γ/2) ⋅ w2σ2
(3)

where

CE(w)
is the certainty equivalent return of the portfolio
w
is the fraction held in equities
r
is the safe rate
μ − r
is the equity risk premium
γ
is risk aversion, from equation (2)
σ2
is the variance of equity returns

The gain term w(μ − r) is linear in the position. The cost term is quadratic. Reward therefore grows in a straight line while the penalty accelerates, so there is exactly one position where they balance. Differentiating and setting to zero:

w* = (μ − r) / (γ ⋅ σ2)
(4)

where

w*
is the optimal share of total wealth in equities
μ − r

γ
is risk aversion
σ2

Source: Merton (1969). Samuelson (1969) derives the discrete-time equivalent.

Hold equities in proportion to the reward for risk, and in inverse proportion to the amount of risk and the investor's dislike of it.

Note that the numerator in equation (1) is written ln(1+μ) − ln(1+r). Under geometric Brownian motion an annual return quoted as μ corresponds to a drift of ln(1+μ), and the Merton numerator is a difference of drifts. The source spreadsheet uses μ − r directly. Measured on the spreadsheet's own three sleeves, that overstates the numerator by 3.6%.

2.2 Why volatility is squared

Equation (3) penalises variance, not volatility, because to second order only the mean and variance of the return enter an expected utility calculation. The consequence is an asymmetry: doubling the risk premium doubles the position, while doubling volatility cuts it to a quarter. The result is about twice as sensitive to the volatility estimate as to the return estimate.

2.3 Why time horizon does not appear

Equation (4) contains no term for holding period. Two households with the same γ hold the same share of financial wealth in equities whether they are 30 or 70.

This is a result, not an omission, and it requires two conditions: returns independent from year to year, and constant relative risk aversion. Under both, the multi-year problem reduces to repeating the one-year problem, which is why the solution is called myopic (Samuelson, 1969).

The common contrary argument observes that long-run returns are more predictable. That observation is correct but concerns the wrong quantity. Let annual log returns be independent and normal with mean m and standard deviation s. The standard deviation of the cumulative return is s√T, which grows with the horizon, while the standard deviation of the annualised average is s/√T, which shrinks. At m = 5% and s = 16%, on $10,000, with 90% intervals:

The annualised range narrows and the spread of outcomes widens. A household consumes the ending balance, not the annualised average. The third column is the scale-free version of the second: it removes the effect of compounding, which would otherwise widen any dollar range mechanically, and it still triples.

On the 90%. The interval is a presentation choice, not a result, and it is worth being explicit about it. A 90% range covers outcomes a household might actually experience; a wider one runs into the tails, where the assumption that returns are normally distributed is least reliable and where the numbers would carry a false air of precision. Widening it to 95% changes every printed figure, moving the ratio in the last column from 5.3 and 17.9 to 7.3 and 31.0, and changes no conclusion. The width of the log range is 2z⋅s⋅√T, so whatever z you pick, the range grows by exactly √3 between ten years and thirty. A measure that needs no interval at all behaves the same way: the coefficient of variation of ending wealth, √(exp(s2T)−1), goes from 0.54 to 1.07 over the same extension.

Two further points close the argument.

The narrowing carries no information. The annualised range narrows by a factor of √(T2/T1), an expression containing the two horizons and nothing about the asset. Every investment narrows at the identical rate, so the measurement cannot distinguish a Treasury bill from a penny stock.

The gap between assets never closes. Because the narrowing factor is the same for both, comparing two assets over the same horizon leaves their ratio fixed.

The ratio holds at 3.20, which is 16 divided by 5. Time rescales both assets identically and therefore contributes nothing to the choice between them. A longer horizon would favour equities only if it narrowed their range faster than it narrowed the alternative's, which it does not.

Two supporting results point the same way. Bodie (1995) shows that the cost of insuring an equity position against underperforming cash rises with the horizon, from 20.0% of the amount invested over ten years to 33.9% over thirty at 16% volatility; if long horizons reduced equity risk, that insurance would get cheaper. And the probability of underperforming cash does fall with the horizon, from 27.7% to 15.2% on the same parameters, but the average shortfall when one occurs nearly doubles, leaving the expected shortfall flat.

2.4 The empirical question

All of the above assumes returns are independent from year to year. Whether they are is empirical and unsettled.

Fama and French (1988) and Poterba and Summers (1988) find a slowly mean-reverting component in long-horizon returns, and Siegel documents that thirty year annualised real returns in United States data have dispersed less than independence implies. Against this: two centuries of data contain only about seven non-overlapping thirty year periods, so the estimate is weak, and the rolling windows normally used overlap heavily. Dimson, Marsh and Staunton show the United States was among the strongest markets of the twentieth century, so a conclusion drawn from it alone selects on the outcome. Pástor and Stambaugh (2012) find that once parameter uncertainty is included, predictive variance per year rises with the horizon. Barberis (2000) contains both effects and finds the optimal equity share does rise with horizon, but by much less than mean reversion alone implies.

Version 1 assumes independence, which sits between the two positions. The ratios themselves are now measured, on Shiller's real total return series, against the volatility of one-year returns rather than against monthly returns scaled up. That distinction matters: his monthly price is an average of that month's daily closes, and averaging smooths, so scaling his monthly series by the square root of twelve reads 14.25% where one-year returns read 18.30%, understating by 22%.

Read naively this is alarming for the model. Merton's share is proportional to one over the variance, so if the volatility that matters over thirty years is 8.42% rather than the 17.18% the model is fed, the model is understating the equity share by a factor of four. That reading is wrong, for two reasons that are worth separating.

The first is that Merton's sigma is not a horizon. His model runs in continuous time with a constant investment opportunity set, and its sigma is the instantaneous volatility of the price process. Annualised daily returns estimate that. Substituting a thirty-year annualised figure would not be a better estimate of the same quantity; it would be a different model, the one Campbell and Viceira (2002) build, in which returns are predictable and the optimal share carries a hedging term Merton's does not have. This tool implements Merton as Choi implements him, and the ratios above are evidence that the constant-opportunity-set assumption is imperfect, not evidence that the input is wrong.

The second is that the sign is not settled. Pastor and Stambaugh (2012) make the argument directly against the reading above: once an investor admits they do not know the expected return, and the ratios above are computed as though it were known, uncertainty about the mean compounds with horizon and long-run variance comes out higher than the independent-returns scaling, not lower. Section 3.1 is a demonstration of exactly the uncertainty they mean, spanning 7.05% to 2.42% across defensible estimates of the same number. The honest position is that the horizon effect on variance has a disputed sign, and that assuming independence sits between the two camps rather than favouring either.

Section 6 gives the practical size of this question: the age-based rule that the horizon argument produces costs 2.00% of lifetime consumption against 0.06% for the approach used here.

What Choi specifies

Nothing. The expected return is a user input in his spreadsheet, and his guide calls it "your best guess of the average return of the stock market over your lifetime in excess of the inflation rate". He offers a default of 5% with a one-sentence justification: it is "roughly equal to what is implied by current stock market valuation ratios if those ratios stay constant and future dividend or earnings growth equals its long-run historical average".

That sentence is a construction rather than a number, and it is the construction implemented here. There is no other method of his to adopt.

Today: a 1.10% dividend yield plus 2.29% real growth in earnings per share, no repricing, giving 3.38% compound, which is 4.92% as an arithmetic mean.

Why the dividend yield and not the payout yield

This is the one place in the whole model where the obvious choice is wrong, and it was wrong here until it was measured. Cash reaches a shareholder two ways, as dividends and as buybacks, so a total payout yield looks like the more complete measure of income. It is. What it cannot be paired with is per share growth, because retiring shares is exactly what makes earnings per share grow. Counting a buyback as income and then again as growth counts it twice.

A company makes this concrete. It has 100 shares at $15, earns $100, pays $30 of dividends and buys back $20. Aggregate real earnings are flat and the multiple is held constant. The buyback retires 1.33 shares, so earnings per share rise 1.35%, and so does the price. The holder receives $0.30 of dividends on a $15 share and ends holding $15.20 of stock.

On today's figures the error was worth 1.53 percentage points of expected return, which is 12 points of total wealth and, for the default household, 32 points of the equity share: 70% rather than 38%. It is recorded here rather than quietly repaired because the size of it is the argument for reading section 3.1 at all.

This is not a private reading. AQR's published capital market assumptions build the same estimate the same way, with the buyback term entering as an adjustment to growth rather than to the yield, and arrive within half a point of the figure here. Section 8.3 sets the two side by side.

The second row is the honest alternative and it is closed off by the data rather than by the theory. Aggregate earnings growth needs a share count, and the S&P earnings history is per share and carries none. Straehl and Ibbotson (2017) set out the same requirement at length and construct the aggregate series that would satisfy it; Bernstein and Arnott (2003) measure the gap the two rows differ by, and find that per-share earnings grew about two points a year slower than aggregate earnings over the twentieth century, because net share issuance was positive for most of it.

That last point cuts against this estimate, and it should be stated plainly. The growth term is a trend through a century in which companies issued shares on net; the yield term is measured today, when they retire them. Both halves are therefore measured in the regime that suppresses them. If buybacks continue at today's rate, future growth in earnings per share will run above the historical trend, and 3.38% is too low. Adding the full 1.53% back is not the fix, since that is the arithmetic error again, but the direction of the remaining error is known and it is upward.

Why this estimator and not another

Three independent arguments point the same way, which is why one estimator is used rather than a median across several.

It is Choi's own stated rationale. Equation (5) with repricing at zero is his sentence written as arithmetic. Choosing anything else would mean overriding the author of the model on the one input he chose to comment on.

It is what the industry does. The building-block decomposition is the standard form for long-horizon capital market assumptions, used in one variant or another by Vanguard, JPMorgan, BlackRock, GMO and Research Affiliates. It is not a private construction.

It is the method behind Choi's own anchor. He calibrates the paper's central example to AQR's year-end 2023 forecast of a 1.9% log excess return for United States large-cap equities, made, in his words, "using dividend and payout discount models". That is this method. Adopting it estimates the input the same way as the figure the model was demonstrated with.

The alternatives are each rejected for a stated reason.

The implied premium is forward looking and current, and it is the outlier at 7.05%, which alone would put the default household at 100% equities. It is a discounted cash flow on the index, so it inherits the growth forecasts fed into it, and those are analyst estimates over a few years rather than a sustainable long-run rate: Damodaran's own workbook carries an expected growth rate near 14% in the month used here. It is also quoted against the 10-year nominal Treasury while this model's safe asset is a 30-year real bond, a maturity mismatch worth roughly half a point at the current shape of the real curve. For a lifetime horizon these are the wrong properties.

The valuation regression fits realised subsequent 30-year real returns on the cyclically adjusted earnings yield across Shiller's history and reads off today's ratio. It is the Campbell and Shiller (1988, 1998) relation and a genuine empirical finding, but as an estimator it is weak: an R2 of 0.19, and although it has 1,387 overlapping windows they contain only about four independent thirty-year periods, leaving a standard error near 0.74 percentage points. It is also the only component that needs a long series to be current, which is the dependency this tool is least able to guarantee.

The earnings yield is the closest competitor and the case for it is better than it looks. Earnings are earnings however they are distributed, so unlike equation (5) it is untouched by the shift from dividends to buybacks that makes both of that equation's terms hard to measure in the current regime. It is also equation (5) under the assumption that retained earnings earn exactly the cost of equity, so retention neither creates nor destroys value; where that holds the two agree. Trailing, it gives 3.96% and 50% equities. Cyclically adjusted, one divided by CAPE, it gives 2.42% and 19%. The two differ by a point and a half, which is the argument against it as a single number: the trailing version collapses in any recession, and the cyclically adjusted version rests on a ten-year average that is itself the thing in dispute. The table below shows it beating equation (5) at ten and twenty years and losing to it at thirty.

A historical average, near 7% real, is rejected because a century of annual data still leaves a standard error of 1.5 to 2 percentage points on the mean, and because it ignores the price being paid today.

How growth is estimated, and why as a trend

The series is monthly real earnings per share on the S&P 500, running back to 1871. A hundred years of it is 1,200 monthly observations. The question is how to turn those into one annual growth rate, and there are two obvious ways.

Point to point. Take the first observation and the last, and ask what constant annual rate connects them:

g = ( Elast / Efirst )12 / (n − 1) − 1
(6)

On the current data that is real earnings of $15.29 in July 1923 and $181.77 in June 2023, which gives 2.51% a year. Note what has happened: 1,198 of the 1,200 observations were ignored. The answer is decided entirely by two months, and it inherits whatever the earnings cycle happened to be doing in each of them. July 1923 was six years before a crash; June 2023 sat near a post-pandemic earnings peak. Neither month was chosen for any reason except that it is where the window happens to end.

The trend. Take the logarithm of every observation, fit a straight line through all 1,200 of them by ordinary least squares, and read the slope:

ln Et = a + b⋅t + εt      g = e12b − 1
(7)

where

Et
real earnings per share in month t
b
the fitted slope, the average monthly growth in log earnings
e12b − 1
that monthly slope expressed as an annual rate

Logarithms are taken first because growth is multiplicative: a constant percentage growth rate is a straight line in logs, and a curve in levels. Fitting a line to the levels would let the large recent numbers dominate the small early ones and would not answer the question asked. On the current data the trend gives 2.29% a year.

Both describe the same century. They differ by 0.34 percentage points because one is an average over 1,200 months and the other is a measurement of two.

That column bought this estimate its margin of safety, and it was needed. For a time the tool read the series from a community mirror that stopped carrying the consumer price index in September 2023. Every deflated column is computed from that index, so all of them went to zero at once while nominal prices kept arriving, and the growth estimate silently rested on data three years old. Because the estimator is a trend rather than two endpoints, the cost of that was about a basis point rather than most of a percentage point.

A source that fails any of those is passed over for the next one, and if none answers the refresh stops rather than writing something it cannot stand behind.

The ten-year average at each end is shown because it is the obvious middle option: it replaces each endpoint with an average of the 120 months around it. It is much better than point to point and still worse than the trend, for the same reason, that it uses 240 of the 1,200 observations rather than all of them.

Compound is not arithmetic

Equation (5) yields a compound return, because it is a Gordon discount rate. The model's input is a level, that is arithmetic, return. The paper states the conversion itself when translating its own parameters, giving a level equity premium of exp(r + π + σ2/2) − exp(r) = 3.86% for a log premium of 2%. So the estimate is converted once, at the point it is handed to the model:

μarithmetic = exp( ln(1 + μcompound) + σ2/2 ) − 1
(8)

At 17.2% volatility that is worth 1.54 percentage points: 3.38% compound becomes 4.92% arithmetic. Feeding the compound figure straight in would understate the input by more than the entire equity risk premium is currently worth, since the premium over a 2.96% real safe rate is 1.96 points and the adjustment is 1.54.

What this leaves unresolved

Two things, stated rather than hidden.

Setting repricing to zero is an assumption, and not a neutral one. A market priced well above its own history has a negative expected repricing term that this construction ignores, so the estimate should be read as generous rather than conservative.

At today's values the resulting log excess drift is 0.17%, below the 2% to 4% grid the approximation's coefficients were fitted on, and the safe rate is above its own grid as well. Section 3.6 sets out what those grids are, why they are where they are, and what being outside them does and does not affect.

The user can override all of it. The estimate is a default rather than a claim to know the number, and both front ends expose it directly.

3.3 The safe rate

Taken as a real rate directly, from the 30-year Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities yield, which is what Choi's guide asks for. Deriving it instead as a nominal yield less an inflation expectation would mismatch maturities, since the freely available series are a 3-month bill and a 10-year breakeven and their difference is neither a 3-month nor a 10-year real rate. Where bonds sit in a taxable account the guide reduces the rate by the marginal income tax rate.

Which point on the real curve, and what it costs

This is the second most expensive choice in the model and it was previously made in one sentence. Forty-three points of the equity share sit between the top row and the bottom one. The reason is arithmetic: the Merton numerator is the gap between the equity drift and the safe drift, and moving the safe rate from 1.37% to 2.96% removes 1.56 points from a gap that is only about two points wide to begin with. When the premium is thin, the choice of safe asset stops being a detail.

The long bond is the right answer, and the argument is Campbell and Viceira's. For an investor with a short horizon, cash is riskless and a long bond is risky, because its price moves. For an investor with a thirty-year horizon this reverses: a long inflation-indexed bond delivers a known real amount at the date the money is needed, while cash has to be rolled at rates nobody knows, which is reinvestment risk. The riskless asset is defined by the horizon, and this model's horizon is a lifetime. Choosing cash here would also be choosing the higher answer, which is a reason to be careful rather than a reason to do it.

Why this is not offered as a choice

Having measured that the safe asset is worth 43 points, the obvious next move is to let a reader pick their own maturity. It would be a mistake, and the reason is visible in the table above: every step towards a shorter maturity raises the recommended equity share. That looks backwards, and the appearance is instructive.

The model does not ask how risky the safe asset is. It asks what the safe asset pays, and then compares equities against it. A shorter real yield is a lower one, on 96% of the days since 2010 and by an average of 0.97 points between the five and thirty year points, so naming a shorter maturity names a worse alternative and equities win by more. A menu of maturities would therefore be a dial for manufacturing a higher equity share, presented as a technical detail. Nobody would pick the 30-year twice.

The deeper objection is that the shorter options are not riskless for this household at all. The model has one safe rate, constant and certain. A thirty-year inflation-linked bond held to maturity delivers exactly that. Cash, or a five-year bond rolled, delivers an unknown sequence of rates, and feeding its current yield to a model that treats it as certain forever is assuming away the reinvestment risk that is the whole difference between them. The 81% on the cash row is not a more aggressive reading of the same facts; it is an answer to a question with a false premise in it.

So the maturity stays fixed, and what the tool does instead is say on its face what the safe asset is and what it pays. A reader who sees "38% in equities" and nothing else reads it as pessimism about the stock market. A reader who sees that the alternative is 2.96% above inflation, guaranteed for thirty years, is looking at the actual trade-off.

The one thing this weakens is the fitted grid, twice over. A higher safe rate lowers the drift and raises the safe rate itself above the grid the coefficients were fitted on, which section 3.6 sets out in full. Section 3.1 reports a log excess drift of 0.17%, well below the 2% to 4% values Choi solved at. Some of that distance is the safe asset rather than the equity market: Choi anchors his central 2% example to AQR's 1.9% log excess return, and AQR quotes excess of cash. Measured the same way, on cash, this model's drift is 1.73%, which is a quarter point below the band rather than 1.8 points below it. That inference rests on AQR's convention rather than on a statement in Choi's paper, so the tool continues to report the drift against its own safe asset and to warn, but the warning should be read as partly a statement about the shape of the real curve and not only about how thin the equity premium is.

3.4 Risk aversion

γ describes the household, not the market. It cannot be estimated from prices and there is no value to look up. Household portfolio studies and experimental elicitation both produce values between 2 and 10, a range wide enough to change the recommendation by a factor of five.

Choi's user guide elicits it with a single question rather than a questionnaire. You face a coin flip: heads you live on $100,000 for the next year, tails on $50,000. A genie offers to cancel the gamble for a guaranteed amount. The amount that leaves you indifferent is the certainty equivalent of the gamble, and under constant relative risk aversion it identifies γ exactly.

An answer of $70,710 is γ = 1, $63,246 is 3, $58,566 is 5, and $53,991 is 10. This project computes those figures from equation (2) rather than storing the guide's table, and inverts the relation so that any answer maps to a value.

This replaces the willingness, ability and need scoring an earlier draft of this document used. That scoring is advisory practice; the question above is the definition of the parameter the model contains. The scale is 1 to 10 because the layer two discount rate equations take γ/10 as an input and were fitted on it. Section 6 shows this is the input that matters most.

3.5 Which equity market, and why not a global one

Everything above estimates the expected return of the United States stock market, and the volatility in section 3.2 is measured on an S&P 500 tracker. A reasonable question is whether the tool should offer developed-market or global alternatives instead. The honest answer is that the equity side could be done and the rest of the model could not, and offering the choice would advertise a generality the model does not have.

What would be straightforward

Volatility. This is already a parameter. The refresh reads market_ticker from the configuration and estimates volatility from five years of that instrument's dividend-adjusted closes. Pointing it at a developed-market or all-country tracker instead of an S&P 500 one is a one-line change to the configuration file and needs no new code.

What would not

The expected return. The estimator is a dividend yield plus a hundred-year growth rate for real earnings per share. The first has regional equivalents. The second does not, at least not freely: Shiller's series is United States only, and there is no comparable free century-long earnings-per-share history for developed or global markets. The recognised source for long international records is Dimson, Marsh and Staunton, and it is not public.

The tempting shortcut is to pair a regional dividend yield with the United States growth rate, on the argument that long-run real earnings growth reflects global productivity and should not differ much by region. That argument is weaker than it sounds. Real earnings per share have grown at visibly different rates in the United States, Europe and Japan over the periods for which records exist, and a difference of half a point in growth is worth more to the answer than most of the other inputs combined. It would be a guess wearing the clothes of a measurement.

The constraint that actually decides it

The model is calibrated to the United States on the side that has nothing to do with equities. Four things are fixed inside the fitted coefficients and cannot be changed from any interface:

  • the earnings profile and the variances of permanent and temporary income shocks, estimated by Cocco, Gomes and Maenhout on United States household panel data for college graduates;
  • the survival probabilities, from United States mortality statistics;
  • the 40% retirement benefit replacement rate, which describes Social Security;
  • the correlation between labour income and the stock market, estimated against the United States market.

Offering a global equity option while those four stay fixed would produce a household with Japanese or European equity exposure, American wages, American mortality and an American state pension. That is not a more general tool; it is an inconsistent one, and the inconsistency would sit on the side the user cannot see.

A non-United States user's first problem is not the equity index. It is that the human capital half of the model, which contributes most of the recommendation for anyone still working, describes somebody else's country.

What is offered instead

The expected return can be set directly, in both front ends and from the command line. Anyone holding a developed or global portfolio, or working from a house forecast, can enter it. That is the same latitude Choi gives, since the input is a user's best guess in his spreadsheet too, and it does not pretend to a regional estimate the free data cannot support.

3.6 The grid the coefficients were fitted on

The tool warns that its inputs sit outside a fitted range. That warning is worth reading properly, because the range is not a recommendation, it is not a band of sensible values, and it is not one range but two.

What it actually is

Choi solves the underlying model numerically, at particular parameter values, and then fits regressions to the answers. Section 1.2 of the paper lists the values. Risk aversion takes 3 values, the permanent and temporary shock variances 3 each, the replacement rate 3, the earnings path 7, and the two that concern us here:

log real risk-free rate    0, 0.01, 0.02
log risk premium          0.02, 0.03, 0.04
 

Crossed, those give 7 × 36 = 5,103 parameter sets. So "the 2% to 4% band" is three numbers, not an interval: the model was solved at exactly 2%, 3% and 4% and nowhere else. Everything between them is already interpolation, and everything outside is extrapolation.

Why those values, in his words

He picked them deliberately low. The paper says they are "considerably lower than the 6.5% log equity premium realized from July 1926 to July 2024", and anchors the bottom of the range to a forward-looking estimate rather than to history: the 2% case is "similar to the 1.9% forecast for U.S. large-cap equities' log excess return over cash made by the hedge fund AQR at year-end 2023 using dividend and payout discount models".

The top of the range he justifies explicitly, and the bottom he does not. On the top: "even a 2% log equity premium results in optimal equity allocations that are frequently 100%. Therefore, any approximation that accurately fits the solution for log equity premia of 4% or less should be accurate for log equity premia above 4%." Once the answer saturates at 100%, accuracy above that point stops mattering. There is no equivalent argument going down, because a lower premium moves the answer into the interior where the approximation has to work, and he had no reason in 2025 to think a reader would face a premium below AQR's 1.9%.

Note also that AQR's 1.9% was quoted over cash, which is what section 3.3 turns on.

5. Layer two: valuing human capital

Everything in layer two reduces to one question: what discount rate applies to a wage to be received in twenty years?

Not the safe rate, and the reason is not the obvious one.

The intuitive story, that wages fall when markets fall, is not what drives this. Choi cites an estimated correlation between real labour income growth and real S&P 500 returns of 0.007 for the average household. Effectively zero. The baseline model assumes labour income is uncorrelated with the stock market, and that assumption is precisely why human capital behaves like a bond rather than like equity.

What makes a future wage worth less than a Treasury paying the same amount is that the household cannot get rid of the risk. Two features of the setting do the work:

  • The risk cannot be diversified or sold. A salary is specific to one person and there is no market in it. In a complete market, risk uncorrelated with everything else would command no premium at all, because anyone could diversify it away. Here nobody can, so the household bears all of it and values the income accordingly.
  • It cannot be borrowed against. Future wages are near certain to arrive but unavailable now, and the model forbids borrowing against them. Income you cannot reach is worth less than income you can.

So the gap between roughly 9.7% and a 2% real Treasury yield is not payment for market exposure. It is the price of holding an undiversifiable, unsellable, unborrowable claim. This follows from the market being incomplete, and it is the reason a full life-cycle model is needed rather than a discount rate read off an asset pricing formula.

No formula gives that rate. Obtaining it means solving the full life-cycle problem: consumption, saving and portfolio choice in every year of an eighty year life under uncertain wages, returns and lifespan, with no ability to borrow. Cocco, Gomes and Maenhout (2005) solved it numerically by backward induction. The output is a table of policies, not an equation.

Choi, Liu and Liu (2025) solved that model for 5,103 parameter sets, then fitted closed-form expressions to the resulting discount rates. This delivers the numerical result at the cost of one line of arithmetic, and adopting it rather than re-solving the model is the highest-value decision in this design.

One detail about how these rates are built. Present value at any age therefore compounds the chain of one-year rates between here and the year in question, rather than applying a single rate to everything.

6. Accuracy, and what the alternative rules cost

Choi, Liu and Liu report both figures a user should want: how closely the approximation tracks the exact numerical solution, and how much worse the common rules of thumb are.

They report two different things, and the distinction matters.

Allocation accuracy: how close is the percentage? They solve the exact model at every point of a grid of 5,103 parameter sets, 78 ages and 201 wealth levels, then regress the true optimal equity share on the approximation. That gives an R2 of 0.99, a slope of 0.95 and an intercept of 0.05, with a root mean square error of 3.66 percentage points. Two thirds of parameter sets come in under 4 points and 95% under 5.

Welfare: does the difference matter? Allocation error is the wrong final measure, because section 7 shows the objective is flat near its optimum, so a few points of error can cost almost nothing. So they also compute expected lifetime utility under the approximate policy and under the exact one, and express the gap as the reduction in constant lifetime consumption that would produce the same loss of utility. That is the figure in Table 17, measured from the standpoint of a 22 year old whose only wealth is the current year's income.

A known bias. The slope of 0.95 is not noise. The approximation is somewhat too aggressive where the true optimum is below 83% equities, and slightly too conservative above it. The authors note that multiplying the output by 0.946 would reduce the error further. This tool does not apply that correction, because it is a post-hoc fit to their own residuals rather than part of the model, but a user near the middle of the range should read the recommendation as very slightly high.

What is not reported, and would be worth having, is the worst case rather than the average. The 0.06% is a mean across parameter sets; the per-γ columns in Table 17 are the closest thing to a tail figure and they show the spread is modest for this rule and very large for the alternatives.

7. Declared deviations

7.1 Conflicting volatility assumptions

The problem. Equity volatility appears in two places and they disagree. Equation (4), the Merton share, needs the volatility of the portfolio you actually hold, currently measured at about 17.2%. Equations (9) and (10), the discount rates, have 18.5% baked into their coefficients, because that is the value the underlying model was solved at.

What the tool does. It uses your measured figure in equation (4) and leaves 18.5% inside the discount rates.

Why not make them agree. Both ways of doing so are worse. Feeding your figure into the discount rate equations would use a fitted approximation outside the range it was fitted over, which is not a small liberty, since those coefficients have no standing away from the grid they were estimated on. Feeding 18.5% into equation (4) would knowingly use the wrong volatility for the portfolio the recommendation is actually about.

What it costs. Little. Volatility reaches the discount rate only through the π term, worth −0.32 points of the 9.7% in Table 16, and human capital then enters the answer as a ratio to financial wealth, which dilutes a small valuation error further. The seam is declared because it exists, not because it is dangerous.

7.2 United States calibration

The constants in Table 17 come from a calibration to United States household earnings, and the Social Security treatment is specific to that system. The equity sleeve is global. The tool is calibrated for a household in the United States; applying it elsewhere would require re-estimating these constants.

7.3 Labour income correlated with equities

Who this affects. Anyone whose pay moves with the stock market: equity compensation, a trading seat, commission tied to markets, a job at an asset manager. Not the average household, whose wages correlate with the market at roughly 0.007, as section 5 explains.

Why it matters for them. If your income already rises and falls with the market, part of your human capital is an implicit equity holding rather than an implicit bond. Counting it as a bond, which the baseline does, tells you to buy more equities on top of exposure you already have.

The fix exists and we have not implemented it. Choi, Liu and Liu give it as their equation (19): subtract (βperm + φ⋅βtran) ⋅ HC/W from the equity share, where the betas come from regressing your income shocks on stock returns. They test it and report a welfare loss of 0.02%. This tool omits it only because the spreadsheet it was built from omits it. It is listed in Table 19 as implementation work, not research.

Until then. If your pay is market-linked, treat the recommendation as too high, by roughly the fraction of your human capital that behaves like equity.

The horizon is not matched, and matching it moves this model from slightly high to slightly low. Every peer forecast above runs seven to twenty years; this one runs a lifetime. 8.4 Whether the answer discriminates

A recommendation that comes out at 100% for everyone is not advice. Across a grid of 1,040 households spanning ages 25 to 85, wages of $40,000 to $250,000, wealth of $25,000 to $3,000,000 and risk aversion of 2 to 8, 30.3% of answers are pinned at 100% and none at 0%, leaving about seven in ten interior. Before the buyback correction, 40.0% were pinned. The saturation is concentrated where it should be, among the young, whose human capital dwarfs their savings, and where the model's own honest answer is that the constraint against borrowing binds.

8.5 What has not been checked, in order of what it would cost

Uncertainty in the expected return. The model treats a point estimate as known. Section 3.1 shows that estimate spanning 7.05% to 2.42% across methods that are all defensible, a range worth the entire allocation. Barberis (2000) shows that admitting the uncertainty lowers the equity share, and by more at longer horizons, which is the reverse of the usual intuition. This is the largest gap in the model and nothing here compensates for it.

The permanent wage shock volatility. Section 5.2 measures it at 22 points of the answer across its plausible range, second only to the expected return, and it is a constant the user cannot see or change. Validating it would mean re-estimating earnings risk on current data rather than inheriting a 2005 calibration, and ideally letting a household say whether its own earnings are more secure than average.

Anything outside the United States. Every input, and the earnings risk calibration behind the discount rates, is United States data. Section 3.5 explains why a global version is not offered rather than offered badly.

The buyback adjustment, which was measured and rejected. AQR corrects its historical growth rate by the change in buyback intensity: the 10-year net buyback yield minus the 25-year one, on the reasoning that a company retiring shares faster than it used to will grow earnings per share faster than its own history suggests. Doing the same here needs a net buyback yield reaching back as far as the growth window. Building it from Damodaran's payout yield less the dividend yield gives 173 months, from January 2012, which is already inside the buyback era; over that window the 10-year average is 2.37% against a full-sample 2.54%, so the adjustment comes out at minus 0.18 points. That is the opposite sign to the effect it exists to capture, and it is an artefact of a window too short to contain the change it is trying to measure. AQR's own term also travels with a cross-sectional shrink and a growth adjustment that partly offset it, so taking one piece of their construction without the others would not be doing what they do. The gap this leaves is half a point, named in section 8.3, and a documented gap is better than a correction with the wrong sign.

The estimator in a buyback regime. The backtest in section 3.1 runs from 1910, and buybacks only became a material share of payout after 1982. Both terms of equation (5) are measured on a century in which companies issued shares on net, and the test cannot say how the estimator behaves in the regime the tool is now being used in. This is a known limit with a known direction, upward, and no way to size it from this data.

Whether anyone acts on it. No claim is made that a household following this allocation is better off than one following a simpler rule, only that this allocation is the one the model implies. Section 6 reports what the alternative rules cost inside the model, which is a different and much weaker statement.
