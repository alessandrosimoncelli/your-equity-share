What the model does not do, and what each omission is worth

Version 1.1, October 2026. Companion to the technical methodology, which describes what the model does do.

Every Italian figure here is as of 8 September 2026, the data the Italian methodology was written with, and is recomputed by the tools named beside it. Equity shares are for the default household of the methodology: age 45, 1,500,000 of financial wealth, 100,000 of salary, risk aversion 5, on the Italian configuration, which recommends 54.5%, before tax as the model works (Italian methodology, section 8). American figures are on the data of 3 September 2026, the American methodology's.

This is the list of things the model deliberately does not do. It exists so that finishing can mean finishing rather than forgetting, and so that anyone reading the tool can see the shape of what is missing rather than discovering it later.

Every entry carries a number, or says plainly that it does not have one yet. An omission with no measured cost is a wish rather than a plan, and it is impossible to prioritise a list of wishes. Where the number is absent it is because measuring it is itself part of the work.

1. The Italian variant

1.1 Tax, measured and left out

Decided on 2 October 2026. The Italian answer leaves the tax on returns out, as Choi's model does: it has no taxes and treats every quantity as implicitly after tax, and his guide adjusts only the safe rate, for bonds held in a taxable account. Italian law taxes the equity fund at 26% and a government bond fund at 12.5%, on the gain at sale, but a fund held for years is usually in gain when sold, 81% of the time at seven years and 97% at thirty, so the tax takes a share of the risk as well as of the return. Taken through the law sale by sale (tools/tax_check.py), it lowers the right equity share by about a quarter for money sold at three years, by about a ninth at seven and by under a tenth from twelve, and ignoring it costs under two basis points a year at three years and under half a basis point from seven. The variant had applied tax to the expected returns only, which cut the share by 15% to 28%: close to the law at three years, and from seven between two and a little over four times its cut. Italian methodology, section 8.

What would settle it beyond a single decision is the full life-cycle model with Italian tax, carrying each fund's cost basis. It is not needed to ship and is recorded here rather than built.

1.2 The deferral horizon, no longer needed

Taxing the expected returns needed a date of sale, set first at thirty years and then at the sooner of thirty years and the household's expected lifetime. With tax left out of the model there is no sale date to choose.

1.3 Pension wrappers

A tax regime of its own, which the model does not carry any more than it carries the ordinary one, and for a household that uses it, it is probably larger than everything else in this document. Italian pension funds are taxed on returns at 20% rather than 26%, with 12.5% on the government bond share; contributions are deductible from income up to 5,164.57 euro a year; and the benefit is taxed at 15%, falling by 0.3 points a year from the sixteenth year of membership to 9% after thirty-five.

It is deferred because it changes the asset menu rather than the rates. A household choosing between a fund inside a pension wrapper and the same fund outside one is answering a different question from the one this model asks, and the deductibility in particular interacts with income rather than with wealth.

What the page does already is count the balances at what they will pay out. Its savings hint asks for a pension fund, including TFR paid into one, at about nine tenths of its value, for the tax on the benefit, and for TFR left with the employer at about three quarters, for its separate tax. That is the tax on the way out, which belongs in the savings figure; the lower tax on the way through and the deduction are what this item would add.

1.4 Shares rather than funds

Gains on funds are redditi di capitale and losses on the same funds are minusvalenze, which are redditi diversi, and Italian law does not let the two be netted. Gains on individual shares are redditi diversi too, so there the losses do offset.

With full loss offset the state has taken a stake in the position and shares the downside as well as the upside, the Domar and Musgrave (1944) result. Section 8 of the Italian methodology measures the fund case, where losses do not offset, and finds the same thing in practice: a fund held for years is usually sold at a gain (81% of the time at seven years, 97% at thirty), so the tax still takes a share of the swings. A household holding individual shares is in the offset regime, which the model does not represent either; tax is left out in both cases.

1.5 The human capital calibration

The career and the pension are now Italian: Daminato and Padula's earnings process for private employees, estimated on the Bank of Italy's household survey, and the Ragioneria Generale dello Stato's 66.4% replacement rate. That rate is a share of final net pay without the TFR, and the page asks for the wage with the yearly TFR accrual in it, so the model imputes the same pension as 61.8% of that wage (Italian methodology, section 7.2). What remains American is inside Choi's coefficients: the 2019 United States life table, and the American earnings parameters his discount rates were fitted on, Cocco, Gomes and Maenhout's estimates for three education levels, combined independently.

Making those Italian would mean refitting the coefficients on Italian data rather than substituting inputs, which is why it is not a configuration change.

One part of the pension is measured and not coded. The Ragioneria's 66.4% is for a career whose pay keeps pace with average pay, and a contributory pension pays a career that rises faster less against its final wage: a career growing half a point a year faster takes the 2050 rate from 60.5% to 56.1% of gross pay (Ragioneria Generale dello Stato, 2026, Table 6.2.a). If the whole rise in Daminato and Padula's age profile were individual career growth, the rate would be about 51% rather than 61.8%, which lowers the answer by about 1 point for the default household, by about 1 to 2 points for workers aged 40 to 50 with ten years of pay saved, and by up to about 4 just below 100% (Italian methodology, section 7.2). That is the size if the whole slope is a career effect. How much of it is, the survey data cannot say precisely, which is why it is recorded here rather than coded.

1.6 Italian inflation rather than euro area

The safe rate is the ECB's AAA 30-year par yield less the market break-even of the Bund€i, so it is real against euro area inflation, HICP. An Italian household's cost of living is Italian, and only BTP Italia tracks that, at five years and retail. So the deflator is approximate in a way an American's TIPS is not.

1.7 Currency

The growth term is real growth in United States earnings per share, read as a euro real rate. That holds if relative purchasing power is preserved, which is AQR's own stated assumption for reading local real returns in any currency. It is defensible over a lifetime because real exchange rates mean-revert over decades. It is not defensible over five years, and nothing in the model represents the real currency losses a euro investor in an unhedged world fund can experience for long stretches. The volatility, 16.45%, is measured on the index priced in euro (Italian methodology, section 6), so the currency's month-to-month swings are inside it; a decade-long drift of the real exchange rate is not.

A hedged share class would remove that risk at a cost, and the model has no way to express the trade.

1.8 Withholding tax

Done on 2 October 2026. Withholding costs a world fund 0.34 points of dividend yield as MSCI's net index measures it, taken at source before the dividend reaches the fund, so no Italian holder ever receives it. It is a property of the fund, like its price, and not the household's own tax. So the Italian expected return uses the yield net of withholding, 1.19% against 1.53% gross, which makes it 3.50% compound rather than 3.85%. An American holding domestic stock suffers none, so the gross Shiller column is the same measurement there. The American page's world-index figure, for an American who holds a world fund, is printed on the gross yield instead, to match the S&P figure beside it; a fund based in the United States suffers no withholding on the American part of the index and some on the rest, so for its holder the right figure lies between the gross and the net one (American methodology, section 3.5). The Italian page tells a reader who enters a forecast quoted gross, such as AQR's, to take about 0.3 points off it.

The fund itself pays less than MSCI deducts. MSCI applies the rate a fund pays without a tax treaty, 30% on American dividends, the largest single country's share of the index's, where VWCE, in Ireland, pays 15% on them. The fund's own costs take about as much back: over five to seven years to August 2026 its price rose 0.06 to 0.07 points a year less than the net index (Italian methodology, section 4.1). So the Italian figure is in effect after the fund's costs, while the American one is before any fund's costs, as in Choi's guide. A 0.03% index fund would take the American answer for the default household from 20.7% to 20.4%. Kept here so the numbering of the items after it does not move.

1.10 The growth term is inherited, and it leans one way

The Italian variant measures its own dividend yield on a global index but takes its growth term from the United States, because no free source carries a century of global earnings. That substitution has now been measured rather than assumed, in tools/us_vs_global.py, and it is not neutral.

The direction is settled and the size is not. Every estimate that is not built on a biased construction says the United States grew faster than the rest of the world, so the global growth term is too high. How much too high is open: AQR's published pair implies a gap costing 0.10 points on the global rate, and the modern MSCI sample, twenty-three years in local currency, implies 1.48.

What limits it is arithmetic rather than either estimate. The global index is about 64% American, so an American growth rate is only wrong on the other 36%: a full point of annual difference between the United States and everywhere else costs 0.36 points on the global rate. On the two usable estimates, the growth term is too high by between 0.10 points, on AQR's pair, and 1.48, on MSCI from 2002 to 2024, a short window of unusual American outperformance. This project reads it as a tenth to half a point, which is a judgement and not a measurement. Each 0.1 point of growth is worth about 1.7 points of equity share for the default household, and 1.2 to 2.2 points for the other uncapped Italian households tried: half a point would take the default household from 54.5% to 46.0%, and the full 1.48 points to 29.8%.

AQR's figure is a lower bound rather than a best guess, because their construction pulls each country's growth towards common figures: a cross-sectional shrink towards the global average, a shift a third of the way towards forecast GDP growth, and an average with an equilibrium rate of 1.8% shared by every country (American methodology, section 8.3). Their tight gap is partly their own method.

The estimate is not optimistic against the firms either. Measured gross of withholding, as AQR measure theirs, it is 3.85% compound, 0.35 points below AQR's 4.20% for the same index at the end of 2025; the model uses it net of withholding, 3.50%. A firm that has the global data and needs no American substitute arrives higher, not lower.

It is not corrected, because correcting it would mean inventing a haircut. Scaling by AQR's ratio looks principled and is not: they publish to one decimal, so their 2.7% and 2.6% fit an American lead over the rest of the world anywhere from 0 to 0.56 points, which is 0 to 0.20 on the global rate. An adjustment less reliable than the thing it adjusts is not an improvement.

2. Both variants

2.1 The horizon audit of the peer table

Table 26 of the methodology sets this model against nine published forecasts. Every one of them except Damodaran's, which is perpetual, runs five to twenty years, and this model prices a lifetime, so the comparison is not like for like. The correction is noted there in prose and never carried into the number: Vanguard, the only firm publishing both horizons off one model run, has its thirty-year forecast 0.9 points above its ten-year.

2.2 A better global growth series

Both variants currently use one number, the hundred-year trend through Shiller, and for the Italian one that is a United States growth rate standing in for a global one. It is the largest borrowed thing left in the model. AQR's pair, 2.7% for United States large cap against 2.6% for Global All Country, puts its cost at a tenth of a point on the global rate, but their construction pulls country figures together, so that is a lower bound; the modern MSCI sample puts it at 1.48 points (section 1.10). Our trend sits 0.31 points below their global figure. The sign of the error is known and its size is not.

tools/global_growth.py is the record of trying to remove it and failing. Rebuilding global real dividend growth from free long-horizon data moves the answer 4.5 points across weighting schemes that are equally defensible, because a fixed basket of countries is not an index: an index reconstitutes, and reconstitution cannot be reproduced from country index returns.

What would fix it is a global series with earnings in it. That is the rare ingredient. Long price histories are common and long earnings-per-share histories are not, which is the whole reason Shiller's file is the one being used.

The sources were surveyed in September 2026 and the result is recorded here so the search does not have to be repeated. It is not that global index earnings do not exist. It is that every series long enough is paid, and every free one either has no earnings in it or gives sorted portfolio returns rather than earnings levels.

MSCI's free end-of-day service was probed directly: it answers for index levels and returns 404 for every fundamentals endpoint tried. Kenneth French's international portfolios are formed on earnings to price and cover 21 countries from 1975, but they publish the portfolios' returns rather than the ratio itself, so no earnings level can be recovered from them.

Buying the data would help less than it sounds. The longest paid global series starts around 1980, which is a 46-year window. The American estimator is stable from about seventy years upward and comes apart below sixty: on Shiller's own data it reads 2.28% at 69 years, 2.48% at 60, 3.11% at 50 and 3.98% at 40, as the window shortens onto the recent earnings boom. A 46-year global window would sit inside the range where the estimator is known not to work, which is the same reason the twenty-five years of MSCI data already in hand swing 2.37 points across start dates.

So the honest statement of the position is not that an American series is being used because it is American. It is being used because it is the only equity earnings series anywhere that is long enough for a long-horizon trend, and the substitution it forces is one whose direction is known and whose size is bounded only loosely, between 0.10 and 1.48 points of growth (section 1.10).

Two routes could still change that. A vendor opening a global fundamental history back to 1970 or earlier, which MSCI could do since their index starts in 1969. Or national accounts: cap-weighted corporate profits from the OECD reach back to the 1960s for most members, but they are aggregate rather than per share, and the dilution adjustment needed to convert them is about as uncertain as the quantity being estimated. Bernstein and Arnott put that wedge near two points a year for the twentieth century.

2.3 The American safe rate is at a historical extreme

This is context rather than a defect, and it belongs here because it is the single largest thing driving the American answer and nothing on the page says so.

FRED's 30-year TIPS yield was 2.96% on 3 September 2026. Across its own history, 4,138 daily observations from February 2010, when the series begins, it had a minimum of −0.59%, a median of 1.01% and a maximum of 3.06%, so the day sat at the 99.6th percentile. By 29 September it had risen to 3.29%, a record for the series. Long TIPS paid more before the series began, around 2000, and for a few days in late 2008, so the record is the series' and not the asset's. These are the yields as quoted, on the Treasury's semiannual basis; the model reads them annualised, so the 2.96% enters it as 2.98%.

So the American variant recommends 21% equities to a 45-year-old on $100,000 with $1.5 million saved partly because its safe asset is paying close to the most it has since 2010. The model is right to use the rate the market offers today, and section 3.3 argues that case at length. But a reader seeing 21% should know that the number is being held down by a real risk-free rate at the top of its recent range. Each tenth of a point the rate falls adds about a point to that household's answer, and the series' median of 1.01% comes from a decade of low real rates, so it is no forecast of where the rate is going.

Nothing needs fixing. What is missing is the sentence saying it, on the page rather than only here.

2.4 A stale Shiller file holds back the safe rate

A known limitation of the weekly refresh, recorded before it has ever happened. update.py refuses to save when Shiller's history is more than twelve months old, or older than the one already saved, because a dividend yield that old saved under today's date would be worse than keeping last week's figures. The refusal stops the whole American refresh, so the 30-year TIPS yield is not updated that week either, and because the two variants are refreshed together or not at all, the euro safe rate waits too. Every week the history stays stale, both pages keep the safe rates of the last good week.

What it costs is the safe rate's own movement while it lasts. On the American snapshot each tenth of a point of TIPS yield moves the default household by about 1 point of equity share, and the page's own default, 45 on $100,000 with $500,000 saved, by about 2. The third of a point the yield rose between 3 and 29 September 2026 is worth 3.4 points to the first and 6.5 to the second. Nothing binds today: the history runs to June 2026. The fix is to refuse only the expected-return fields, keep last week's, and still write the new safe rate.

3. Not on this list, and why

Two things were considered and rejected rather than deferred, and they are recorded here so they do not come back as suggestions.

Historical average returns, for either the expected equity return or the safe rate. Choi does not use them: his stated rationale for the 5% default is current valuation ratios, held constant, with growth at its long-run historical average, which is the building block the model implements. The backtest leans the same way without settling it: a flat 5%, the one constant in Table 7 of the methodology, has a larger error than the estimator used here at every horizon, by 0.3 to 0.5 points of root mean square error, though at thirty years it beats the two earnings yields, and three independent thirty-year windows cannot separate figures that close. And the literature rejects them, most directly in Fama and French (2002), who show that estimates from dividend and earnings growth are far more precise than estimates from average returns, and that the high realised average of the second half of the twentieth century came from an unexpected decline in discount rates, which is a one-off capital gain rather than a repeatable expected return.

A more frequently published capital market assumption. AQR publish once a year and the houses that publish quarterly or monthly all assume valuations revert towards a fair value, which is a different estimator rather than a fresher number. Switching for the sake of frequency would change the method. The two halves of the estimate are checked against free data on every run instead.
