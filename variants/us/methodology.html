1. Approach and scope

This tool implements the approximation in Choi, Liu and Liu (2025), which builds on Merton (1969). The structure has two layers.

Layer one gives the share of total wealth that belongs in equities. This is the Merton share, covered in sections 2 and 3.

Layer two accounts for the fact that most of a working household's total wealth is the present value of wages not yet earned, which cannot be sold or borrowed against. It converts the layer one answer into an instruction about the tradeable portion. This is Bodie, Merton and Samuelson (1992), implemented through an approximation fitted to the numerical solution of Cocco, Gomes and Maenhout (2005). Sections 4 and 5.

Combined, the calculation is one expression:

wfin = clip( [ln(1+μ) − ln(1+r)] / (γ ⋅ σ2) × (1 + HC/W), 0, 1 )
(1)

where

wfin
is the share of the investable portfolio to hold in equities, the figure the tool reports
μ
is the expected annual return on the equity portfolio, after inflation
r
is the annual return on a safe asset, also after inflation
γ
is relative risk aversion, on a scale of 1 to 10
σ
is the annual volatility of the equity market, held at Choi's 18.5% (section 3.2)
HC
is human capital: the present value of future wages, Social Security benefits and any other pension
W
is investable financial wealth today
clip
holds the result between 0 and 1, ruling out shorting and leverage

The first factor is the Merton share. The second, 1 + HC/W, is total wealth divided by financial wealth. The clip belongs to the model the formula approximates: Cocco, Gomes and Maenhout's household can neither borrow nor sell short, and Choi's equation (9) carries the same two limits.

The source spreadsheet this tool replaces computes only the first factor. Choi's spreadsheet computes the whole expression but with simpler inputs: one equity asset, a fixed 5% expected return, no valuation input. This tool takes the input estimation from the former and the human capital treatment from the latter. It is calibrated for a household in the United States, for the reason given in section 7.4.

1.1 Who this is for

The model answers one question and it is worth naming, because a reader who brings the wrong question to it will get a confident answer to something they did not ask.

It answers: of the money a United States household has invested and does not need on any particular date, what share belongs in equities rather than in a safe bond. It assumes wages or a pension are coming in, and that the money is meant to last the rest of a life. It also assumes the equities are a broad holding, such as an index fund: Choi's guide says the recommendation "does not hold" for a portfolio of "only a small number of stocks", and the page says so at the top.

It is calibrated for a college-educated American household whose Social Security will replace about 40% of final pay. Forty percent is the median Biggs and Springstead find for new retirees in the highest fifth of lifetime earnings; lower earners are replaced at more. Above a final wage of about $100,000 after tax the program's largest benefit binds, and the share replaced falls below 40% (section 7.7). Table 1 below lists every number behind that description and where it comes from. A household that knows its own benefit can type it in the pension field with the age it will claim, ticked as its Social Security, and it then replaces the 40% (section 7.6).

It does not answer anything with a date on it. A house deposit in three years, school fees in eight, a known payment on a known day: none of that fits, and the reason is structural rather than a missing feature. Section 2.3 shows that horizon does not appear in the Merton share at all, which is a result and not an omission. A model in which horizon does not appear cannot distinguish money needed in three years from money needed in forty, so it cannot be asked to. Goal-based allocation is a different problem with a different solution, and this tool has nothing to say about it.

It also does not say how much to save, which securities or funds to hold, or what to do about a house, a business, a mortgage or an annuity. Section 8.5 lists what is left out and what each omission costs.

Nor does it answer a household with nothing invested, or with debts larger than its savings. Choi's guide says its authors "do not have the model's solutions" when financial net worth is negative, and the page then shows no share: it says that Choi's model has no answer when savings are zero or debts exceed them.

What "no fixed date to spend it" rules out

Money you will spend on a known day does not belong here, and not because of a missing feature. Section 2.3 derives the reason: the horizon is not one of the model's inputs, so a deposit due in three years and a pension pot forty years out are the same problem to it. There is no mechanism by which its answer could differ between them.

The practical reading: type in what you have invested as Choi's guide defines it, non-housing assets less non-mortgage debt, after the tax that will be owed on withdrawal, less any money set aside for a dated goal, such as a house deposit, a car, a wedding or a tax bill. For a pre-tax 401(k) or IRA balance the guide suggests counting 0.8 of it when the future tax rate is not known, and the hint under the field says so.

In the paper's equations (5) to (7) the household enters each year with its savings, receives that year's income, chooses how much of the total, its cash on hand, to spend, and invests the rest (sections 1.1 and 1.3 of the paper). What is invested is therefore savings plus this year's income less this year's spending, which is savings as they stand whenever this year's income pays for this year's spending. That is why a worker types savings as they stand. A retiree whose pension falls short of this year's spending leaves out only the shortfall, the part that will come out of savings this year. The years of spending after that stay in: drawing savings down over the rest of a life is the model's own consumption, and the money that will be spent later is invested until then.

The answer is for today. Run it again once a year and rebalance to the new figure, using new savings first; the page says so under the result. The rule is meant to be applied at every age. The welfare figures of section 6 are for a household that follows it for life, re-reading it each year as its age and savings change (section 4 of the paper), and the figure usually falls as savings grow and the career shortens.

2. Layer one: the Merton share

The investor has constant relative risk aversion, meaning the percentage of wealth put at risk does not change as wealth changes. Utility is

U(W) = W(1 − γ) / (1 − γ)
(2)

where

U(W)
is utility from ending with wealth W
W
is final wealth
γ
is relative risk aversion, controlling how sharply utility flattens as wealth rises

The flattening is what produces risk aversion: each additional dollar adds less utility than the one before, so a gain does not offset an equally likely loss of the same size. Higher γ means faster flattening.

2.1 The trade-off

Place a fraction w of wealth in equities and 1 − w in the safe asset. Over a short period, maximising expected utility is equivalent to maximising the certainty equivalent, the guaranteed return the investor would accept in place of the uncertain one. To second order:

CE(w) = r + w(μ − r) − (γ/2) ⋅ w2σ2

Hold equities in proportion to the reward for risk, and in inverse proportion to the amount of risk and the investor's dislike of it.

Note that the numerator in equation (1) is written ln(1+μ) − ln(1+r). Under geometric Brownian motion an annual return quoted as μ corresponds to a drift of ln(1+μ), and the Merton numerator is a difference of drifts. The source spreadsheet uses μ − r directly. Measured on the source spreadsheet's own three equity sleeves, that overstates the numerator by 3.6%.

Why a premium near zero still buys equities

The premium in equation (4) is the average return of equities over the safe rate, not what they compound at in a typical year. Because returns swing, the average sits above the compound rate by about half the variance: at 18.5% volatility, about 1.7 points. A forecast that compounds barely above the safe rate is therefore still an average premium of about 1.7 points, and the formula still puts part of total wealth in equities.

This comes from the model, not from a choice made here. Merton's μ is the expected return, which is the average. Choi writes the same term out in his equation (8): the log premium plus half the variance, divided by risk aversion times the variance. When the log premium is zero, what remains is one over twice the risk aversion: 10% of total wealth at a risk aversion of 5.

The intuition is rebalancing (Booth and Fama, 1992). A stock that rises 20% or falls 15% with equal odds compounds at 1.0% a year. Hold half of it beside a bond paying 1% and rebalance every year, and the mix compounds at 1.37%, faster than either on its own. The one step this project adds is the conversion: its forecast is a compound return (section 3.1), so the page adds half the variance before the formula uses it. The same arithmetic says when the answer reaches zero: only once the forecast falls below the safe rate by more than half the variance.

2.2 Why volatility is squared

Equation (3) penalises variance, not volatility, because to second order only the mean and variance of the return enter an expected utility calculation. The consequence is an asymmetry: doubling the risk premium doubles the position, while doubling volatility cuts it to a quarter. With the premium held fixed as an arithmetic mean, Merton's formula is about twice as sensitive to the volatility estimate as to the return estimate.

That holds for a fixed arithmetic premium, which is not how this tool is fed. Its forecast is a compound return, converted to an arithmetic mean at the same volatility the formula then uses (section 3.1). The numerator of equation (1), the form the tool computes, is then the log premium plus half the variance, so the Merton share splits into π/(γσ2) + 1/(2γ), where π is the log risk premium, ln(1+compound) − ln(1+r). Only the first term depends on volatility, and its size scales with π, which in the United States today is 0.41%, close to zero. Moving the volatility from 17.5% to 19.5% moves the default household from 38.1% to 36.6%. Holding the arithmetic mean at 5.19% instead, the same move takes it from 41.8% to 33.5%.

2.3 Why time horizon does not appear

Equation (4) contains no term for holding period. Two households with the same γ hold the same share of financial wealth in equities whether they are 30 or 70.

This is a result, not an omission, and it requires two conditions: returns independent from year to year, and constant relative risk aversion. Under both, the multi-year problem reduces to repeating the one-year problem, which is why the solution is called myopic (Samuelson, 1969).

The common contrary argument observes that long-run returns are more predictable. That observation is correct but concerns the wrong quantity. Let annual log returns be independent and normal with mean m and standard deviation s. The standard deviation of the cumulative return is s√T, which grows with the horizon, while the standard deviation of the annualised average is s/√T, which shrinks. At m = 5% and s = 16%, on $10,000, with 90% intervals:

Both bounds are stated as compound annual rates, so they reconcile with the ending values beside them. The interval itself is symmetric in logs, which is why the widths in Table 3 stand in exactly the ratio of the two volatilities.

The annualised range narrows and the spread of outcomes widens. A household consumes the ending balance, not the annualised average. The third column is the scale-free version of the second: it removes the effect of compounding, which would otherwise widen any dollar range mechanically, and it still triples.

On the 90%. The interval is a presentation choice, not a result, and it is worth being explicit about it. A 90% range covers outcomes a household might actually experience; a wider one runs into the tails, where the assumption that returns are normally distributed is least reliable and where the numbers would carry a false air of precision. Widening it to 95% changes every printed figure, moving the ratio in the last column from 5.3 and 17.9 to 7.3 and 31.0, and changes no conclusion. The width of the log range is 2z⋅s⋅√T, so whatever z you pick, the range grows by exactly √3 between ten years and thirty. A measure that needs no interval at all behaves the same way: the coefficient of variation of ending wealth, √(exp(s2T)−1), goes from 0.54 to 1.07 over the same extension.

Two further points close the argument.

The narrowing carries no information. The annualised range narrows by a factor of √(T2/T1), an expression containing the two horizons and nothing about the asset. Every investment narrows at the identical rate, so the measurement cannot distinguish a Treasury bill from a penny stock.

The gap between assets never closes. Because the narrowing factor is the same for both, comparing two assets over the same horizon leaves their ratio fixed.

Two supporting results point the same way. Bodie (1995) shows that the cost of insuring an equity position against underperforming cash rises with the horizon, from 20.0% of the amount invested over ten years to 33.9% over thirty at 16% volatility; if long horizons reduced equity risk, that insurance would get cheaper. And the probability of underperforming cash does fall with the horizon, from 27.7% to 15.2% on the same parameters, but the average shortfall when one occurs nearly doubles, leaving the expected shortfall flat.

2.4 The empirical question

All of the above assumes returns are independent from year to year. Whether they are is empirical and unsettled.

Fama and French (1988) and Poterba and Summers (1988) find a slowly mean-reverting component in long-horizon returns, and Siegel documents that thirty year annualised real returns in United States data have dispersed less than independence implies. Against this: two centuries of data contain only about seven non-overlapping thirty year periods, so the estimate is weak, and the rolling windows normally used overlap heavily. Dimson, Marsh and Staunton show the United States was among the strongest markets of the twentieth century, so a conclusion drawn from it alone selects on the outcome. Pástor and Stambaugh (2012) find that once parameter uncertainty is included, predictive variance per year rises with the horizon. Barberis (2000) contains both effects and finds the optimal equity share does rise with horizon, but by much less than mean reversion alone implies.

Version 1 assumes independence, which sits between the two positions. The ratios themselves are now measured, on Shiller's real total return series, against the volatility of one-year returns rather than against monthly returns scaled up. That distinction matters: his monthly price is an average of that month's daily closes, and averaging smooths, so scaling his monthly series by the square root of twelve reads 14.25% where one-year returns read 18.30%, understating by 22%.

Read naively this is alarming for the model. For a given premium, Merton's share is proportional to one over the variance, so if the volatility that matters over thirty years is 8.42% rather than the 18.5% the model is fed, the model is understating the equity share by a factor of almost five. That reading is wrong, for two reasons that are worth separating.

The first is that Merton's sigma is not a horizon. His model runs in continuous time with a constant investment opportunity set, and its sigma is the volatility of the price process per unit of time. When returns are independent, daily, monthly and annual returns all estimate that same number, and over the full history of the S&P 500 they agree to within half a point (section 3.2). A thirty-year annualised figure is a different quantity. Substituting it would not be a better estimate of the same thing; it would be a different model, the one Campbell and Viceira (2002) build, in which returns are predictable and the optimal share carries a hedging term Merton's does not have. This tool implements Merton as Choi implements him, and the ratios above are evidence that the constant-opportunity-set assumption is imperfect, not evidence that the input is wrong.

The second is that the sign is not settled. Pastor and Stambaugh (2012) make the argument directly against the reading above: once an investor admits they do not know the expected return, and the ratios above are computed as though it were known, uncertainty about the mean compounds with horizon and long-run variance comes out higher than the independent-returns scaling, not lower. Section 3.1 is a demonstration of exactly the uncertainty they mean, spanning 7.07% to 2.42% across defensible estimates of the same number. The honest position is that the horizon effect on variance has a disputed sign, and that assuming independence sits between the two camps rather than favouring either.

Section 6 gives the practical size of this question: the age-based rule that the horizon argument produces costs 2.00% of lifetime consumption against 0.06% for the approach used here.

What Choi specifies

Nothing. The expected return is a user input in his spreadsheet, and his guide calls it "your best guess of the average return of the stock market over your lifetime in excess of the inflation rate". He offers a default of 5% with a one-sentence justification: it is "roughly equal to what is implied by current stock market valuation ratios if those ratios stay constant and future dividend or earnings growth equals its long-run historical average".

That sentence is a construction rather than a number, and it is the construction implemented here. There is no other method of his to adopt.

Two things about that figure are worth stating. The terms are compounded, not added. The yield is trailing, last year's dividends over today's price. With valuation ratios held constant, next year's dividend is that dividend grown at the growth rate, and the price grows at the same rate, so the identity is exact in this form: 1 + return = (1 + yield)(1 + growth). Adding the two, as the building-block form of Grinold and Kroner does, drops the cross term, 0.03 points today. It is the return on the index before any fund's costs, as Choi's 5% is, since his guide asks for the return of the stock market itself: an index fund charging 0.03% a year would take 0.5 points off the default household's answer, and SPY's 0.0945% would take 1.7. The Italian variant's figure, built on a net index, is in effect after its fund's costs (Italian methodology, section 4, on the dividend yield), so the two variants differ here.

Why the dividend yield and not the payout yield

This is the one place in the whole model where the obvious choice is wrong, and it was wrong here until it was measured. Cash reaches a shareholder two ways, as dividends and as buybacks, so a total payout yield looks like the more complete measure of income. It is. What it cannot be paired with is per share growth, because retiring shares is exactly what makes earnings per share grow. Counting a buyback as income and then again as growth counts it twice.

A company makes this concrete. It has 100 shares at $15, earns $100, pays $30 of dividends and buys back $20. Aggregate real earnings are flat and the multiple is held constant. The buyback retires 1.33 shares, so earnings per share rise 1.35%, and so does the price. The holder receives $0.30 of dividends on a $15 share and ends holding $15.20 of stock.

This is not a private reading. AQR's published capital market assumptions average two estimates. The first is this one, a dividend yield plus growth in earnings per share, with their buyback term entering as an adjustment to growth rather than to the yield. The second puts buybacks in the yield instead, as half of a cyclically adjusted earnings yield, and pairs it with a fixed long-run growth rate rather than a measured one (their footnote 2). Neither counts a buyback twice, and the average lands about half a point above the figure here. Section 8.3 sets the two side by side.

The second row is the honest alternative and it is closed off by the data rather than by the theory. Aggregate earnings growth needs a share count, and the S&P earnings history is per share and carries none. Straehl and Ibbotson (2017) set out the same requirement at length and construct the aggregate series that would satisfy it; Bernstein and Arnott (2003) measure the gap the two rows differ by, and find that per-share earnings grew about two points a year slower than aggregate earnings over the twentieth century, because net share issuance was positive for most of it.

That last point cuts against this estimate, and it should be stated plainly. The growth term is a trend through a century in which companies issued shares on net; the yield term is measured today, when they retire them. Both halves are therefore measured in the regime that suppresses them. If buybacks continue at today's rate, future growth in earnings per share will run above the historical trend, and 3.41% is too low. Adding the full 1.53% back is not the fix, since that is the arithmetic error again, but the direction of the remaining error is known and it is upward.

Why this estimator and not another

Three independent arguments point the same way, which is why one estimator is used rather than a median across several.

It is Choi's own stated rationale. Equation (5) with repricing at zero is his sentence written as arithmetic. Choosing anything else would mean overriding the author of the model on the one input he chose to comment on.

It is what the industry does. The building-block decomposition is the standard form for long-horizon capital market assumptions, used in one variant or another by Vanguard, JPMorgan, BlackRock, GMO and Research Affiliates. It is not a private construction.

It is the method behind Choi's own anchor. He calibrates the paper's central example to AQR's year-end 2023 forecast of a 1.9% log excess return for United States large-cap equities, made, in his words, "using dividend and payout discount models". That is this method. Adopting it estimates the input the same way as the figure the model was demonstrated with.

The alternatives are each rejected for a stated reason.

The implied premium, added to the real safe rate, is forward looking and current, and it is the outlier at 7.07%, which alone would put the default household at 100% equities. It is a discounted cash flow on the index, so it inherits the growth forecasts fed into it, and those are analyst estimates over a few years rather than a sustainable long-run rate: Damodaran's own workbook carries an expected growth rate near 14% in the month used here. It is also quoted against the 10-year nominal Treasury while this model's safe asset is a 30-year real bond, a maturity mismatch worth roughly half a point at the current shape of the real curve. For a lifetime horizon these are the wrong properties.

The valuation regression fits realised subsequent 30-year real returns on the cyclically adjusted earnings yield across Shiller's history and reads off today's ratio. It is the Campbell and Shiller (1988, 1998) relation and a genuine empirical finding, but as an estimator it is weak: an R2 of 0.19, and although it has 1,387 overlapping windows they contain only about four independent thirty-year periods, leaving a standard error near 0.74 percentage points. It is also the only component that needs a long series to be current, which is the dependency this tool is least able to guarantee.

The earnings yield is the closest competitor and the case for it is better than it looks. Earnings are earnings however they are distributed, so unlike equation (5) it is untouched by the shift from dividends to buybacks that makes both of that equation's terms hard to measure in the current regime. It is also equation (5) under the assumption that retained earnings earn exactly the cost of equity, so retention neither creates nor destroys value; where that holds the two agree. Trailing, it gives 3.96% and 47% equities. Cyclically adjusted, one divided by CAPE, it gives 2.42% and 20%. The two differ by a point and a half, which is the argument against it as a single number: the trailing version collapses in any recession, and the cyclically adjusted version rests on a ten-year average that is itself the thing in dispute. The table below shows it beating equation (5) at ten and twenty years and losing to it at thirty.

AQR's earnings-based anchor is computed and reported, because it is the only alternative here with no fitted coefficient, no window to choose and no free parameter beyond two constants its authors state in the text. It is one over the cyclically adjusted ratio, aged five years forward at equilibrium growth, multiplied by a 50% payout ratio, with 1.8% equilibrium growth added back. On today's ratio it gives 3.12%. It brackets the estimate from below as the implied premium brackets it from above, and it is not used as the estimate itself because it embeds a fixed 1.8% growth assumption rather than measuring growth. Section 8.3 uses it to decompose AQR's published 3.9% into its two halves.

A historical average, near 7% real, is rejected because a century of annual data still leaves a standard error of 1.5 to 2 percentage points on the mean, and because it ignores the price being paid today.

Compound is not arithmetic

Equation (5) yields a compound return, because it is a Gordon discount rate. The model's input is a level, that is arithmetic, return. The paper states the conversion itself when translating its own parameters, giving a level equity premium of exp(r + π + σ2/2) − exp(r) = 3.86% for a log premium of 2%. So the estimate is converted once, at the point it is handed to the model:

μarithmetic = exp( ln(1 + μcompound) + σ2/2 ) − 1
(8)

At Choi's 18.5% volatility, which the tool holds fixed, that is worth 1.78 percentage points: 3.41% compound becomes 5.19% arithmetic. Feeding the compound figure straight in would understate the input by those 1.78 points, which is most of the equity premium: over the 2.98% real safe rate the arithmetic premium is 2.21 points, and it would shrink to 0.43.

The one step the bond yield does go through, annualising the Treasury's semiannual quote (section 3.3), is a different matter: it changes how often the rate compounds, not whether it is an average.

The caveat that follows from this. It holds for a bond held to maturity, which is what the model's safe asset is: riskless, with a known real payment on a known date. An ordinary TIPS fund is something else. It is marked to market and never matures, so its value moves with real yields every year, and the longer the bonds it holds, the more it moves. Such a fund is not the riskless asset the model assumes, and the model does not say how much of it to hold. The nearest a reader can come to the model's safe asset is TIPS bought and held to maturity, or a ladder of them.

What this leaves unresolved

Two things, stated rather than hidden.

Setting repricing to zero is an assumption, and not a neutral one. A market priced well above its own history has a negative expected repricing term that this construction ignores, so the estimate should be read as generous rather than conservative.

At today's values the resulting log excess drift is 0.41%, below the 2% to 4% grid the approximation's coefficients were fitted on, and the safe rate is above its own grid as well. Section 3.6 sets out what those grids are, why they are where they are, and what being outside them does and does not affect.

The user can override all of it. The estimate is a default rather than a claim to know the number. The page takes it directly, and the command-line tool reads it from its configuration file.

The tool does not measure volatility. It holds it at 18.5%, Choi, Liu and Liu's own figure: the annualised standard deviation of the monthly log return of the CRSP value-weighted index in excess of the one-month Treasury log return, July 1926 to July 2024, as section 1.2 of the paper defines it.

Why it is held fixed. A volatility measured over a trailing window moves the answer whenever a crash enters or leaves the window, with no change in the long-run risk the model is about. In the United States today the figure also matters little, because the forecast is converted to an arithmetic mean at the same volatility: a point of volatility moves the default household by under a point (section 2.2).

3.3 The safe rate

Taken as a real rate directly, from the 30-year Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities yield, which Choi's guide calls a reasonable number to enter. Deriving it instead as a nominal yield less an inflation expectation would mismatch maturities, since the freely available series are a 3-month bill and a 10-year breakeven and their difference is neither a 3-month nor a 10-year real rate. Where most bonds sit in a taxable account the guide suggests reducing the rate by the marginal income tax rate.

The yield is annualised before the model reads it. Treasury par yields, real ones included, are quoted on a semiannual bond-equivalent basis: the 2.96% quoted on 3 September 2026 means 1.48% each half year. The model reads ln(1 + r) as an annual rate, so the tool converts the quote first, (1 + y/2)2 − 1, which is 2.98%. The Italian variant also feeds the model an annual rate, converting its own from the continuously compounded basis on which the European Central Bank quotes it. Choi's guide asks for the yield as quoted, and his spreadsheet takes the logarithm of one plus the figure as typed. Table 21 sizes the difference: on its own the conversion lowers the default household's answer from 37.7% to 37.3%, 0.4 points (1.1 at 35 on $100,000 with $200,000 saved). The compounding of section 3.1 raises it by about as much, so on the default household the two changes offset.

The guide's tax rule understates the tax. The yearly increase in a TIPS principal is itself taxable income in the year it occurs (Internal Revenue Service, Publication 1212), so the tax falls on the real yield and the inflation together, and what is left is roughly r(1 − t) − t⋅i, where t is the tax rate and i is inflation. At a 3% real yield, 2.5% inflation and a combined 30% rate that is about 1.4%, not the 2.1% the guide's rule gives.

This tool does not apply the guide's rule. It uses the pre-tax yield for every household, because an American household's tax depends on the account its bonds sit in: inside a 401(k) or an IRA the pre-tax yield is also the after-tax one, and the page does not ask where the bonds are held. The rule is not small. For the default household at a 24% bracket it lowers the safe rate from 2.98% to 2.27% and raises the answer from 37.3% to 52.7%. The fuller treatment above, with the 2.35% ten-year breakeven as inflation, gives a safe rate of 1.70% and 66.6%. A household whose bonds sit in a taxable account should therefore read the tool's answer as low, and the safe-rate row of the page's Exhibit 5 says so beside the rate: the guide's rule, that the page does not apply it, and that the answer then reads low. The guide taxes only the bond; a tax on the equities in the same account would pull part of the way back. The Italian variant works before tax too, although there the rates are set by law for every resident: taken through the law sale by sale, a tax on a fund held for years takes a share of the risk as well as of the return. It lowers the right equity share by about a quarter for money sold at three years, by about a ninth at seven and by under a tenth from twelve, and ignoring it costs under two basis points a year (Italian methodology, section 8, from tools/tax_check.py on the Italian snapshot).

The long bond is the right answer, and the argument is Campbell and Viceira's. For an investor with a short horizon, cash is riskless and a long bond is risky, because its price moves. The riskless asset is defined by the horizon, and this model's horizon is a lifetime. For a retiree that lifetime is shorter and the matching bond shorter too; section 8.6 sets out what that does to a retiree's answer. Choosing cash here would also be choosing the higher answer, which is a reason to be careful rather than a reason to do it.

Why this is not offered as a choice

Having measured that the safe asset is worth 38 points, the obvious next move is to let a reader pick their own maturity. It would be a mistake, and the reason is visible in the table above: every step towards a shorter maturity raises the recommended equity share. That looks backwards, and the appearance is instructive.

The model does not ask how risky the safe asset is. It asks what the safe asset pays, and then compares equities against it. A shorter real yield is a lower one, on 96% of the days since 2010 and by an average of 0.97 points between the five and thirty year points, so naming a shorter maturity names a worse alternative and equities win by more. A menu of maturities would therefore be a dial for manufacturing a higher equity share, presented as a technical detail. Nobody would pick the 30-year twice.

The deeper objection is that the shorter options are not riskless for this household at all. The model has one safe rate, constant and certain. A thirty-year inflation-linked bond held to maturity delivers exactly that. Cash, or a five-year bond rolled, delivers an unknown sequence of rates, and feeding its current yield to a model that treats it as certain forever is assuming away the reinvestment risk that is the whole difference between them. The 76% on the cash row is not a more aggressive reading of the same facts; it is an answer to a question with a false premise in it.

So the maturity stays fixed, and what the tool does instead is say on its face what the safe asset is and what it pays. Under the answer the page names it, "inflation-linked Treasury bonds (TIPS) held to maturity, or a ladder of them, at the rate in Exhibit 5", and Exhibit 5 gives the 30-year TIPS yield. A reader who sees "37% in equities" and nothing else reads it as pessimism about the stock market. A reader who sees that the alternative is 2.98% above inflation, guaranteed for thirty years, is looking at the actual trade-off.

The one thing this weakens is the fitted grid, twice over. A higher safe rate lowers the drift and raises the safe rate itself above the grid the coefficients were fitted on, which section 3.6 sets out in full. Section 3.1 reports a log excess drift of 0.41%, well below the 2% to 4% values Choi solved at. Most of that distance is the safe asset rather than the equity market: Choi anchors his central 2% example to AQR's 1.9% log excess return, and AQR quotes excess of cash. Measured the same way, on cash, this model's drift is 1.99%, under a hundredth of a point below the band rather than 1.6 points below it, or 1.91%, nine hundredths below, with the bill rate on a bond-equivalent basis (note b). That inference rests on AQR's convention rather than on a statement in Choi's paper, so the tool continues to report the drift against its own safe asset and to warn, but the warning should be read as partly a statement about the shape of the real curve and not only about how thin the equity premium is.

3.4 Risk aversion

γ describes the household, not the market. It cannot be estimated from prices and there is no value to look up. Household portfolio studies and experimental elicitation both produce values between 2 and 10, a range wide enough to change the recommendation by a factor of five.

Choi's user guide elicits it with a single question rather than a questionnaire. You face a coin flip: heads you live on $100,000 for the next year, tails on $50,000, and you must spend the whole amount and cannot borrow. A genie offers to cancel the gamble for a guaranteed amount. The amount that leaves you indifferent is the certainty equivalent of the gamble, and under constant relative risk aversion it identifies γ exactly.

An answer of $70,710 is γ = 1, $63,246 is 3, $58,566 is 5, and $53,991 is 10. This project computes those figures from equation (2) rather than storing the guide's table, and inverts the relation so that any answer maps to a value.

This replaces the willingness, ability and need scoring an earlier draft of this document used. That scoring is advisory practice; the question above is the definition of the parameter the model contains. Section 6 shows this is the input that matters most among those a household controls.

The 1 to 10 scale is the guide's, not the paper's, and this document conflated them until it was checked against the source. The guide publishes a table of ten answers, one for each whole number from 1 to 10, and says economists "typically think that this number should be between 1 and 10". The paper solves the model at 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9 and 10, which is the seven in 7 × 36 = 5,103. So risk aversion is the third axis of the grid in section 3.6, and values below 4 are extrapolation.

They are extrapolation on the benign side, by the same argument Choi makes for the equity premium: "we find that at a risk aversion of 4, the optimal equity allocation is very frequently at the 100% upper boundary", so below 4 the answer saturates and accuracy stops mattering. The tool accepts 1 to 10 because that is what the guide asks a reader for, says so beside the answer when it implies less than 4, and does not claim the approximation was fitted there.

The page asks it in three steps, following what the studies comparing elicitation methods agree on.

First, a self-assessment: in financial matters, how willing are you to take risks, from 0 to 10. This is the question of Dohmen, Falk, Huffman, Sunde, Schupp and Wagner (2011), validated against paid lottery choices, and the second item of Falk, Becker, Dohmen, Huffman and Sunde's module (2023). Self-assessments are the most stable measure of risk attitude over time and the one that best matches risk-taking in everyday life (Frey, Pedroni, Mata, Rieskamp and Hertwig, 2017; Mata, Frey, Richter, Schupp and Hertwig, 2018), and among the questions advisers ask, the best predictor of the portfolio a client prefers (Guillemette, Finke and Gilliam, 2012). It does not enter the model, because no study converts its scale into Choi's. It is used to notice answers that contradict each other: very willing yet cautious in the choices, or very unwilling yet relaxed, which the page then points out.

Second, five choices between the coin and a sure amount, shown one at a time, each amount depending on the answer before, with no counter, no range of answers and nothing preselected, and each presented as standing on its own. That is the staircase of Falk and co-authors, the first item of their module; together with the self-assessment it was the best predictor of real-money risk choices among the roughly thirty survey measures they tested. It replaced a slider over Choi's ten amounts, which showed every answer at once and started at 5. Where a slider starts pulls answers towards it (Liu and Conrad, 2019), and so does the middle of a displayed menu (Beauchamp, Benjamin, Laibson and Chabris, 2020).

Third, a check. The page states the sure amount the five choices imply and asks whether it sounds right, with the option to adjust it: Choi's own question, asked after the choices and anchored on them rather than on an amount the page proposes. A staircase cannot recover from one careless answer, and methods that allow for mistakes are markedly more accurate than those that do not (Chapman, Snowberg, Wang and Camerer, 2024); the check is where a careless answer is undone.

The coin pays what the household lives on now, after tax, or half of it. That is the wage, the second adult's wage and any pension either adult already receives, added up, or the guide's own $100,000 when nothing is coming in. Both percentages are the guide's: its $100,000 is the wage of the paper's worked example and its $50,000 half of that. Framing the gamble on the respondent's own income is how the Health and Retirement Study asks its income gambles, on "current total family income" (Barsky, Juster, Kimball and Shapiro, 1997; Kimball, Sahm and Shapiro, 2008), and how Hanna, Gutter and Fan (2001) ask theirs, on take-home family income; a 50% cut is one of the Study's own gambles. Under constant relative risk aversion only the ratio of the two outcomes matters, so the guide's table holds at any income as shares of the good outcome: 70.7% means 1, 58.6% means 5 and 54.0% means 10.

Each sure amount is the coin's worth at the middle of what the answers so far leave open. The worth comes from equation (2). The first amount, 62.8% of the good outcome, is the coin's worth at a risk aversion of 3.2, the middle of 1 to 10 in ratio terms, and it sits next to the middle of the range of amounts, 62.4%, which is where the staircase of Falk and co-authors starts. Taking the sure amount says the coin is worth less than that, so risk aversion is higher and the next amount is lower; taking the coin moves it the other way. On a $200,000 income, a household that always takes the sure amount sees $125,500, $115,200, $111,100, $109,400 and $108,700, and one that always takes the coin sees $125,500, $135,000, $138,600, $140,100 and $140,800. Each answer halves the range in ratio terms, which holds the equity share, proportional to 1/γ, to the same precision at every level: five answers narrow the range to within about 4% of risk aversion anywhere from 1 to 10. That is the resolution of the questions, not the accuracy of the measurement: it says how finely five answers can place someone who answers without error, not how close a real answer is to the person's true risk aversion. Amounts are rounded to a thousandth of the income's order of magnitude, $100 on a six-figure income. Until the steps are answered the page uses 5, the value Choi, Liu and Liu's spreadsheet comes set to, and says so.

The answers themselves are noisy, and the noise is not corrected. Falk and co-authors (2023) report a one-week test-retest correlation of 0.76 for their two-item risk module, and Kimball, Sahm and Shapiro (2008, section 4) show that such response error raises the average of measured risk tolerance, 1/γ, which is what the share moves with. On this document's data, an error of that size (about 0.41 in log risk aversion, given the spread of true log risk tolerance Kimball, Sahm and Shapiro estimate, 0.73) leaves the typical answer unbiased but raises the average share by 1.5 to 5 points for the households tried below the cap, from the default household to a retiree. Near the cap it lowers the average, because the cap cuts off the upside: by about 14 points for a household of 35 on $80,000 with $150,000 saved and a true risk aversion of 5. At the top of the scale an answer can only err downwards, and the same household at a true 10 gets about 12 points more on average. The 0.76 is for the two items together, while the page feeds only the staircase to the model, so these figures may understate the error. It is not corrected, because a correction needs a population prior and none exists for this tool's users.

The question keeps the guide's conditions and offers no status quo. The whole amount must be spent and nothing can be borrowed, so a bad year cannot be smoothed away. Hanna, Gutter and Fan add the same kind of condition to the Study's question, no other income and no help from anyone, because the answer measures risk aversion only if the outcome has to be lived with. Neither option is to carry on as now: the choice is between two new situations, as in the Health and Retirement Study since 1998, which rewrote its question as a choice between two new jobs because offering the current job as one of the options made risk tolerance look 11% lower (Kimball, Sahm and Shapiro, 2008).

3.5 Which equity market, and why not a global one

Everything above estimates the expected return of the United States stock market, and the volatility in section 3.2 is Choi's figure for the United States market. The S&P 500 stays the market the tool is calibrated on. A reasonable question is whether the tool should offer developed-market or global alternatives instead. For an American household that holds a world index fund the answer is a figure it can type in, set out below. For a household outside the United States the equity side could be done and the rest of the model could not, and offering the choice would advertise a generality the model does not have.

What would be straightforward

Volatility. This is already a parameter: one fixed figure in the configuration file, stock_volatility. Another market needs that market's own figure in its place, and no new code. The Italian variant does exactly that, with 16.45%: Choi's estimator, the annualised standard deviation of monthly log returns, on the MSCI All Country World index in euro from January 2001 to August 2026, measured from every day of the month from the 1st to the 28th and averaged, because month-ends alone give 13.99%, below all 28 of those figures in this window (Italian methodology, section 6). It rests on 25 years where Choi's rests on 98.

What would not

The expected return. The estimator is a dividend yield plus a hundred-year growth rate for real earnings per share. The first has regional equivalents. The second does not, at least not freely: Shiller's series is United States only, and there is no comparable free century-long earnings-per-share history for developed or global markets. The recognised source for long international records is Dimson, Marsh and Staunton, and it is not public.

The shortcut is to pair the other market's dividend yield with the United States growth rate. For a world index it is an approximation with a measured cost, and the Italian variant uses it (Italian methodology, sections 4.2 and 4.4). A world index is about 64% American, so an American growth rate is wrong only on the other 36%. Both estimates of the gap that the Italian methodology can measure (its Table 2) say the United States grew faster, so the pairing puts the world figure too high. They do not agree on how much: 0.10 points on AQR's pair of growth forecasts, 2.7% for United States large caps against 2.6% for the global all-country index, which is a lower bound because their method shrinks differences between countries; and 1.48 points on MSCI's local-currency indices from 2002 to 2024, a short window in which the United States did unusually well. This project reads the bias as a tenth to half a point, which is a judgement rather than a measurement. A check on earnings yields, which do not depend on how profits are paid out, finds the yield side of the same pairing running the other way, so the sign of the net error is not settled. For a regional index, Europe or Japan alone, nothing dilutes the gap: real earnings per share there have grown at visibly different rates from the United States over the periods for which records exist, and the shortcut would be a guess wearing the clothes of a measurement.

The constraint that actually decides it

The model is calibrated to the United States on the side that has nothing to do with equities. Table 1 lists every fixed value and where it comes from: the earnings profile and shock variances, the survival probabilities, the replacement rate and the retirement age. None can be changed from any interface. For an American who holds a world index this raises no difficulty on that side: the wages, the mortality and the pension really are American. What changes is the market, and the page lets such a reader change only its expected return, as set out below. For a household outside the United States it does. Pointing the tool at a European or Japanese market while those values stay fixed would produce a household with that country's equities and American wages, American mortality and an American state pension. That is not a more general tool, it is a less coherent one, and a non-United States reader's first problem was never the equity index.

What is offered instead

The expected return can be set directly on the page, and in the configuration file the command-line tool reads. Anyone holding a developed or global portfolio, or working from a house forecast, can enter it. The hint beside that setting tells a holder of a world index fund that Exhibit 5 shows the same construction run on a world index, and that this figure can be entered instead. It is built from the Italian variant's inputs, the world index's dividend yield plus the same hundred-year growth trend (Italian methodology, sections 4.1 and 4.2), but the page prints it before withholding tax, as the S&P figure beside it is: 1.53% compounded with 2.29%, or 3.85%, on the Italian snapshot. The Italian variant itself uses the yield after the tax a fund based in Europe suffers at source, 1.19%, because no holder of that fund ever receives the 0.34 points withheld. A fund based in the United States suffers none on the American part of the index and some on the rest, so for its holder the right figure lies between the two, and the gross one is a little high. The volatility stays at Choi's 18.5% for the American market either way. That is the same latitude Choi gives, since the input is a user's best guess in his spreadsheet too, and it does not pretend to a regional estimate the free data cannot support.

3.6 The grid the coefficients were fitted on

The tool's inputs can sit outside the range Choi's approximation was fitted on. That range is worth reading properly, because it is not a recommendation, it is not a band of sensible values, and it is not one range but two.

What it actually is

Choi solves the underlying model numerically, at particular parameter values, and then fits regressions to the answers. Section 1.2 of the paper lists the values. The permanent and temporary shock variances take 3 values each, the replacement rate 3, and the earnings path 3, one cubic per education level. The three that concern us here are:

relative risk aversion     4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10
log real risk-free rate    0, 0.01, 0.02
log risk premium          0.02, 0.03, 0.04
 

Crossed, those give 7 × 36 = 5,103 parameter sets. So "the 2% to 4% band" is three numbers, not an interval: the model was solved at exactly 2%, 3% and 4% and nowhere else. Everything between them is already interpolation, and everything outside is extrapolation.

Three of this tool's inputs can leave the grid, and two of them sit outside it today. The safe rate goes out the top, the equity premium out the bottom, and risk aversion out the bottom whenever a reader's answers put it below 4: a sure amount above 60.6% of the coin's good outcome, $60,571 on the guide's $100,000. The page says so beside the answer when risk aversion is below 4, the one input that depends on the reader, and adds there that today's safe rate and premium are outside his range too. Those two are the same for every reader and sit outside the grid at today's values, so a note beside every answer would never change; this section is where they are reported.

Why those values, in his words

He picked them deliberately low. The paper says they are "considerably lower than the 6.5% log equity premium realized from July 1926 to July 2024", and anchors the bottom of the range to a forward-looking estimate rather than to history: the 2% case is "similar to the 1.9% forecast for U.S. large-cap equities' log excess return over cash made by the hedge fund AQR at year-end 2023 using dividend and payout discount models".

The top of the range he justifies explicitly, and the bottom he does not. On the top: "even a 2% log equity premium results in optimal equity allocations that are frequently 100%. Therefore, any approximation that accurately fits the solution for log equity premia of 4% or less should be accurate for log equity premia above 4%." Once the answer saturates at 100%, accuracy above that point stops mattering. There is no equivalent argument going down, because a lower premium moves the answer into the interior where the approximation has to work, and he had no reason in 2025 to think a reader would face a premium below AQR's 1.9%.

Note also that AQR's 1.9% was quoted over cash, which is what section 3.3 turns on.

5. Layer two: valuing human capital

Everything in layer two reduces to one question: what discount rate applies to a wage to be received in twenty years?

Not the safe rate, and the reason is not the obvious one.

The intuitive story, that wages fall when markets fall, is not what drives this. Choi cites Wuthenow (2024), who estimates on the Panel Study of Income Dynamics that from 1980 to 2020 the correlation between real labour income growth and the S&P 500's real returns for the average household is 0.007. Effectively zero. The baseline model assumes labour income is uncorrelated with the stock market, and that assumption is precisely why human capital behaves like a bond rather than like equity.

What makes a future wage worth less than a Treasury paying the same amount is that the household cannot get rid of the risk. Two features of the setting do the work:

  • The risk cannot be diversified or sold. A salary is specific to one person and there is no market in it. In a complete market, risk uncorrelated with everything else would command no premium at all, because anyone could diversify it away. Here nobody can, so the household bears all of it and values the income accordingly.
  • It cannot be borrowed against. Future wages are near certain to arrive but unavailable now, and the model forbids borrowing against them. Income you cannot reach is worth less than income you can.

So the gap between roughly 9.7% and a 2% real Treasury yield is not payment for market exposure. It is the price of holding an undiversifiable, unsellable, unborrowable claim. This follows from the market being incomplete, and it is the reason a full life-cycle model is needed rather than a discount rate read off an asset pricing formula.

No formula gives that rate. Obtaining it means solving the full life-cycle problem: consumption, saving and portfolio choice in every year of an eighty year life under uncertain wages, returns and lifespan, with no ability to borrow. Cocco, Gomes and Maenhout (2005) solved it numerically by backward induction. The output is a table of policies, not an equation.

Choi, Liu and Liu (2025) solved that model for 5,103 parameter sets, then fitted closed-form expressions to the resulting discount rates. This delivers the numerical result at the cost of one line of arithmetic, and adopting it rather than re-solving the model is the highest-value decision in this design.

The equation consumes only what sections 3 and 4 already collect: risk aversion, age, the real safe rate and the equity assumptions. Your wage figures determine what is discounted, not the rate applied to it.

Compare the two equations at the γ term: 0.087 for wages against 0.0003 for Social Security. A cautious household marks down a risky salary sharply and an inflation-indexed government annuity not at all. That is the whole difference between the two in one coefficient.

The path over a lifetime has three parts. The wage rate is highest in early life, when a permanent shock has the most years left to compound. It falls to a minimum near age 53. Then it drops sharply at retirement, because the income being discounted stops being a wage and becomes a benefit. The switch comes once, after the last year with a wage, which is Choi's retirement threshold. Social Security is an inflation-indexed claim on the federal government, so it discounts at close to the safe rate and is worth far more per dollar than a wage is. That is a substantial part of why the model tolerates high equity shares late in life: a retiree with a full benefit record still holds a large bond-like asset outside the portfolio.

One detail about how these rates are built. They are one-year-ahead rates, fitted recursively: the paper first estimates the rate a 99 year old applies to age 100 income, then holds that fixed and estimates the rate a 98 year old applies, and works backwards to age 22. Present value at any age therefore compounds the chain of one-year rates between here and the year in question, rather than applying a single rate to everything.

The cubic in a produces the career hump: earnings rise steeply through the thirties, and the typical path peaks at 45 and is 23% lower by 66. With the leading term, the expected path peaks near 49 and is 10% lower by 66. The leading term is a statistical adjustment, not a feature of careers. Wage shocks are multiplicative, so projected wages are lognormal, and a lognormal's mean exceeds its median by half its variance. Adding that half variance converts the typical path into the expected path, which is what a present value requires.

The tool supports one earner or two, each with a wage and a pension. The wage is after tax and includes any employer retirement contribution, such as a 401(k) match, counted at 0.8 if it goes in before tax, which is the guide's rule; the hint under the field says so. Wages are projected through 66, the last working year in Choi's paper and spreadsheet, and for someone of 66 or more who still earns this year's wage is the last. The hint under the pension field says so, and a different working life is typed year by year.

Social Security is imputed, not typed. It is 40% of the last wage, up to Social Security's largest benefit (section 7.7), paid from the year after the last wage for life. Someone who no longer earns has none imputed: their pension is what they type.

The pension field is for a pension whose amount is already fixed, a year of it after tax: one being paid now, even while the person still works, or one that starts at the age typed beside it, such as a survivor's, disability, military or company pension. Discounting switches from the wage rate to the benefit rate once, after the last year with a wage. Section 7.6 sets out where this differs from Choi's spreadsheet and what each difference is worth.

One small departure from the paper is inherited from the spreadsheet rather than made here. The imputed benefit is 40% of the expected final wage, which carries the half transitory variance of equation (14). The paper's equation (18), which describes the model the discount rates were fitted on, sets the benefit off final permanent income and leaves that term out, so the imputed benefit is 2.97% higher than in the paper. It is worth less than half a point of the equity share except close to retirement with little saved: at 65 on $90,000 with one year of pay saved, 100% against 98.0% under equation (18). From a wage of about $100,000 at 65 the ceiling of section 7.7 binds on both and the difference goes.

Two smaller ones come the same way. The salary typed is used as permanent income, as in the spreadsheet, where the paper's equation (17) scales by permanent income itself. If the salary typed is an average year's pay, temporary shocks included, permanent income is lower by half the temporary variance, a factor of 0.971, and the default household's answer would be 0.7 points lower; across the households tried the difference is 0.2 to 0.9 points. And the tool, like the guide, accepts ages of 20 and 21, where the paper's households start at 22, so the wage rates for their first one or two years are extrapolated from the fitted ages; that is worth under a tenth of a point.

6. Accuracy, and what the alternative rules cost

Choi, Liu and Liu report both figures a user should want: how closely the approximation tracks the exact numerical solution, and how much worse the common rules of thumb are.

They report two different things, and the distinction matters.

Allocation accuracy: how close is the percentage? They solve the exact model at every point of a grid of 5,103 parameter sets, 78 ages and 201 wealth levels, then regress the true optimal equity share on the approximation. That gives an R2 of 0.99, a slope of 0.95 and an intercept of 0.05, with a root mean square error of 3.66 percentage points. Two thirds of parameter sets come in under 4 points and 95% under 5.

Welfare: does the difference matter? Allocation error is the wrong final measure, because the objective is flat near its optimum, as equation (15) below shows, so a few points of error can cost almost nothing. So they also compute expected lifetime utility under the approximate policy and under the exact one, and express the gap as the reduction in constant lifetime consumption that would produce the same loss of utility. That is the figure in Table 20, measured from the standpoint of a 22 year old whose only wealth is the current year's income.

Which way the error runs. The slope of 0.946 and the intercept of 0.054 are not noise. In their footnote 15 the authors write that recommendations could be improved "by multiplying our procedure's equity share by 0.946 and adding 0.054", and that they leave the step out because the extra complexity would bring only small welfare gains. Taken together the two numbers raise every answer below 100%, by 0.054 times the distance to 100%: 3.4 points for the default household's 37.3%. Their Figure 6, which plots the average optimal share against the approximate one, says something else for the low end: the approximation is somewhat too aggressive where the true optimum is below 83% equities, and slightly too conservative above it. The paper's two summaries do not agree on the direction below 83%.

A direct solution of the full Cocco, Gomes and Maenhout model run for this project (not published) settles it for inputs like today's, outside Choi's grid. On the data of 3 September 2026, at a risk aversion of 5 and Choi's 18.5% volatility, the formula comes out about 1 to 3 points low for working households on wages below the ceiling of section 7.7, which the full model does not have, except in the band just below 100%, where it comes out high: by up to about 7 points from 30 to 65, and 10 to 12 at 25 and in the last working year (at 66 with a year of pay saved, 93.9% against 82%). For retirees the high band is wider. It sits where the answer comes down from 100%: at two and a half years of pension saved the formula is 8 to 12 points high from 67 to 85, at three years 5 to 7, and just below the cap up to 25 (at 85 with a year and a half saved, 98.9% against 74%). Where that band falls moves with the market and with risk aversion, so no number of years of pension marks it (section 8.6). This tool applies neither correction. A working household should read its answer as slightly low unless it sits just below 100%. A retiree's answer carries other errors, above all the safe rate, which pull the other way, and section 8.6 sets them side by side.

What is not reported, and would be worth having, is the worst case rather than the average. The 0.06% is a mean across parameter sets; the per-γ columns in Table 20 are the closest thing to a tail figure and they show the spread is modest for this rule and very large for the alternatives.

7.2 One volatility for both layers

Where volatility enters. Twice. Equation (4), the Merton share, divides by it. Equations (12) and (13), the discount rates, take it through π, the log risk premium, which undoes the arithmetic mean at that volatility, and their coefficients were fitted with 18.5% in place.

What the tool does. It uses 18.5% in both (section 3.2), so the two layers read one asset. π is computed at the volatility held, which is Choi's own definition: the log risk premium of the risky asset, ln(1+compound) − ln(1+r). His spreadsheet writes 18.5% into both places as well, so this is no longer a departure. Table 21 no longer lists it, and it stays in this section only so that earlier references to it still resolve.

What changed. Earlier versions fed the Merton share a volatility measured on recent prices, about 17.2%, and kept 18.5% inside π. The forecast was then converted to an arithmetic mean at one volatility and turned back into a drift at the other, which took 0.24 points off the drift the coefficients received. Volatility reaches the discount rate only through the π term, worth −0.32 points of the 9.7% in Table 17, so the seam was small, but it was a seam, and fixing the volatility removed it.

7.4 United States calibration

The constants in Table 18 come from a calibration to United States household earnings, and the Social Security treatment is specific to that system. The equity market is the S&P 500, so both layers describe the same country. The tool is calibrated for a household in the United States; applying it elsewhere would require re-estimating these constants.

7.5 Labour income correlated with equities

Who this affects. Anyone whose pay moves with the stock market: equity compensation, a trading seat, commission tied to markets, a job at an asset manager. Not the average household, whose wages correlate with the market at roughly 0.007, as section 5 explains.

Why it matters for them. If your income already rises and falls with the market, part of your human capital is an implicit equity holding rather than an implicit bond. Counting it as a bond, which the baseline does, tells you to buy more equities on top of exposure you already have.

The fix exists and we have not implemented it. Choi, Liu and Liu give it as their equation (19): subtract (βperm + φ⋅βtran) ⋅ HC/W from the equity share, where the betas come from regressing your income shocks on stock returns. They test it and report a welfare loss of 0.02%. This tool omits it only because the spreadsheet it was built from omits it. It is implementation rather than research, since the authors have already derived and tested it.

Until then, and this is larger than it sounds. The subtraction is the beta times HC/W, not the beta alone, and HC/W is about 2 for the default household and higher for anyone younger. So a beta of 0.1, meaning a tenth of your income shocks track the market, costs 20 points of the equity share, and a beta of 0.2 takes the default household from 37% to zero. Anyone with meaningful equity compensation should treat this tool's answer as unusable rather than merely high, until equation (19) is implemented.

7.6 Pensions, and when discounting switches

What his spreadsheet does. Each adult has one benefit cell, which his guide describes as the current after-tax retirement income benefit, "e.g., Social Security payments". When it is not zero, the spreadsheet pays that amount every year from next year in place of the 40% career pension. It also discounts each year at the benefit rate if a benefit is paid in it and at the wage rate if not, so for someone still working a benefit in that cell moves every remaining wage onto the near-riskless benefit rate. His tab with earnings typed year by year uses the benefit rate in each year with a benefit and the wage rate in each year without. A further cell, B23, switches on the spousal benefit for the second adult.

What the tool does. One discount chain per adult for the wage and the Social Security the tool imputes, as in his spreadsheet, switching from the wage rate to the benefit rate once, after the last year with a wage, which is the retirement threshold his paper switches at (section 3.2 of the paper). A pension in the pension field whose amount is fixed, one already paid or one from an earlier job, is riskless: it is divided by the benefit rates from today, from the age it starts, whether that is next year or later. Unticked, it is added to the imputed Social Security; ticked as Social Security, it replaces it. For a retiree the whole path is on the benefit rate either way, and for a household that types no pension and no break nothing changes from earlier versions of this tool except where the ceiling of section 7.7 binds, and his workbook's own cases still reproduce exactly (Table 24). Two such households still differ from his spreadsheet: an adult aged 65, and a couple using the spousal switch, both below.

Why a fixed pension leaves the wage alone. His rule makes the answer jump on a trivial input. A pension of $1 a year typed by the default household moves its whole salary onto the benefit rate and cancels its career pension: on his rule the answer goes from 37.3% to 50.6%, and to 53.0% in his spreadsheet, which also lacks the floor of section 7.3; on this tool's, unticked, it stays at 37.3%. A $20,000 company or military pension paid while working gives 62.1% on his rule (65.3% in his spreadsheet) and 48.8% on this one. His paper points the other way for a different reason: its income process takes Cocco, Gomes and Maenhout's definition of labour income, which counts Social Security and other transfers of the head and spouse (the paper's footnote 5), so a pension paid while working would follow the wage. Counted as part of the wage, the same $20,000 gives 41.9%. The tool treats it as riskless because its amount is fixed, which is how the paper itself reads a retirement benefit: its section 2.2, after Merton (1971) and Bodie, Merton and Samuelson (1992), discounts a risk-free benefit close to the safe rate.

The tick, and why it exists. Social Security pays one benefit: a disability benefit becomes the retirement benefit at full retirement age, and someone entitled to two benefits is paid the larger. A benefit already drawn while still working, through an early claim, a disability benefit or a survivor's benefit, would be counted twice if it were added to the imputed 40%. A worker of 64 on $24,000 after tax who already draws $20,000 of Social Security, with $100,000 saved, gets 59.8% with the tick and would get 76.3% without it. A pension from somewhere else, a company or the military, is paid on top of Social Security, so it is added: a $20,000 one gives the default household 48.8% unticked, and 46.6% if it were wrongly ticked. The same tick takes an estimate of one's own Social Security typed with the age it will be claimed. That estimate is not fixed yet, so it is discounted as the imputed benefit is, on the wage rates until work ends: entered at the amount the tool would impute, it leaves the answer unchanged. The rule holds at every age. Someone of 66 or more who still earns has Social Security imputed from today's wage, added to an unticked pension and replaced by a ticked one, where earlier versions of this tool replaced it with any pension typed: at 67 on $60,000 with $300,000 saved and a $20,000 company pension, 41.8% against 25.8%.

A pension that starts later. The age beside the pension field is when it starts; left empty, the pension is paid from next year. A fixed pension from a set age, such as one from an earlier job entered unticked, is riskless in the same way, so it too is divided by the benefit rates from today, which his spreadsheet has no way to do: his Wage imputed tab pays its benefit cell from next year, and his Full inputs tab discounts every year before the first payment at the wage rate and every year from it at the benefit rate, the wage included. The default household with a $20,000 pension from an earlier job that starts at 55 gets 45.0% here against 46.6% on his rule. The field is the route for an amount that is fixed. Typed in the year-by-year box instead, the same pension rides the household's own chain, at the wage rate until work ends, and gives 40.0%. A ticked estimate of one's own Social Security is not a fixed pension: as set out above, it is discounted like the imputed one it replaces, so entering the amount the tool would impute leaves the answer unchanged, 37.3% for the default household.

The years between the last wage and a later pension typed year by year. They are discounted at the benefit rate, since no wage risk is left in them; his spreadsheet uses the wage rate until the first benefit. A 55-year-old on $100,000 with $500,000 saved who stops work at 62 and will claim $38,936 of Social Security at 67, with both typed in the box, gets 31.7% here against 30.4% on his rule.

The spousal benefit. His switch is a tick under the second adult: they will claim the spousal benefit, half of the first adult's Social Security, instead of a pension of their own. From the year the second adult is 62, once the first adult has claimed, the first adult's Social Security is increased by half, and the second adult has no pension of their own imputed. The half is valued with the benefit it is half of, on the first adult's discount chain: at the wage rate while it rests on a career still to be earned, and on the benefit rates from today where it rests on a Social Security benefit already fixed and ticked. His spreadsheet discounts it on the second adult's chain instead, at the wage rate until the first payment. For the default earner with a partner of 42 who has no wage that is 38.4% here against 38.5% on his spreadsheet, and 37.3% without the benefit; across the couples tried, working or retired, with the second adult up to ten years younger, the two rules stay within 0.7 points. For a one-earner couple on $100,000, with an earner of 35 to 55 and a partner up to three years younger, the benefit is worth 0.5 to 2.8 points with five years of pay saved and 0.2 to 0.9 with fifteen. It is worth more nearer retirement: 4.6 points at five years for an earner of 60 and a partner of 58. Earlier versions had no switch and told such a couple to type the benefit in the second adult's year-by-year box, where it was valued as riskless from today, as if it did not depend on the first adult's career: 41.4% for the default couple, and 2.3 to 3.0 points too much at five years of pay saved.

A break in the middle of a career. Years typed without a wage, followed by more wages, stay on the wage rate here, since the switch comes only after the last wage, and they pay no imputed pension: Social Security starts the year after the last wage, set off that year's wage. The default household with no wage at 50 and 51 gets 34.1% whether the benefit column is left blank or typed as zero, which is also what his Full inputs tab gives for the same years. Earlier versions paid the imputed pension in the first year without a wage, set it off the wage before the break and kept it for life; his Wage imputed formula would do the same (its column F), but that tab cannot type a break.

Someone aged 65 today. His spreadsheet starts the benefit at 66 for an adult aged 65, at 40% of today's wage, alongside the wage for that year, and discounts that year at the benefit rate. It is a quirk of the first row, which tests for an age of 66 or more where every later row retires people at 67. The tool pays the wage at 66 and the benefit from 67, on the wage at 66, as it does at every other age: at 65 on $90,000 with $450,000 saved, 30.0% here against 31.4% on his spreadsheet. The wage is below the ceiling of section 7.7, which his spreadsheet does not have, so the comparison changes only the timing.

7.7 A ceiling on the imputed pension

What his spreadsheet does. It imputes the retirement benefit at 40% of the final wage, with no upper limit. A household of 45 on $200,000 after tax is credited with a pension of about $76,000 a year.

What the tool does. It holds the imputed pension to Social Security's largest benefit: $4,152 a month at full retirement age in 2026 (Social Security Administration, 2026 cost-of-living adjustment), counted at the guide's 0.8 for tax, which is $39,859 a year. The ceiling binds from a final wage of about $100,000 after tax. It applies only to the pension the tool imputes; a pension the reader types, in the pension field or year by year, is used as typed.

Why. Social Security pays nobody more than its maximum, so 40% of a high final wage credits a household with retirement income it will not receive. The default household sits below the ceiling: its projected final wage is $94,602 and its pension $37,841, so its answer is 37.3% either way. A household of 60 on $150,000 with $1,000,000 saved gets 25.3% with the ceiling and 27.6% without it; on $200,000, 28.1% against 32.7%; at 63 on $250,000 with $1,500,000 saved, 21.8% against 28.1%. Because the earnings path rises steeply through the thirties, a young earner's projected final wage is far above today's, and the ceiling binds at 25 from a wage of about $40,000 and at 35 from about $82,000; those households are mostly at 100% equities either way (section 8.4). Those thresholds are on the expected path, which carries the half variance of section 5.3. On the median path, the one a typical career follows, the ceiling binds at 25 from about $58,000 and at 35 from about $109,000.

What it leaves inconsistent. Three things, each measured within the formula. First, equation (12) still receives a replacement rate of 40% for a household the ceiling reaches, although its pension is then a smaller share of its final wage. The rate enters through a coefficient of 0.010, so a household whose ceiling halves its replacement rate to 20% has its wage discount rate set 0.2 points higher than its own pension would give. Fed its own replacement rate instead, each of the three households above would gain 0.1 points or less, one of 50 on $250,000 with $1,000,000 saved would gain 0.4, and a young high earner with little saved up to about 3: 92.5% against 89.4% at 25 on $300,000 with three years of pay saved. This tool leaves the rate at 40%.

Second, the ceiling is applied to one number, 40% of the expected final wage, where the expected pension is an average over careers, some of which reach the ceiling and some of which do not. The default household's expected final wage leaves its pension under the ceiling, but its career reaches the ceiling in about a third of outcomes, so its expected capped pension is $29,022 rather than $37,841, and its answer 36.8% rather than 37.3%. Across the households tried the answer is up to 0.9 points too high on this account, most near the threshold, and the error vanishes where the ceiling binds on almost every path.

Third, where the ceiling binds on almost every path the pension no longer depends on how the career goes, so it is close to riskless, yet it stays on the wage chain until 66, as Choi's equations keep every income on the household's one chain. Moved to the benefit rates from today, as a pension in the pension field is, it would add 0.7 to 2.4 points for high earners of 50 to 63: at 55 on $200,000 with $1,000,000 saved, 34.0% against 31.6%. That one is a choice rather than an error. Nothing in Choi's paper, and no full solution with a ceiling, settles which chain a capped pension belongs on, so the tool keeps his.

What the checks are worth, measured

Everything above is a list of things that pass. That a suite passes is a claim about the suite and not about the model, and this project has twice been shown the difference: a test that asserted two numbers add up, which was true of the wrong two, and a release the entire suite approved while the published page could not load at all. The only honest measurement is to break the model on purpose and count what notices.

56 deliberate defects were introduced one at a time, each a single changed coefficient, constant, boundary or operator, across the Merton share, both discount rates, the earnings path, the calibration, the statistics, the data layer, the input validation and the JavaScript port. After each, the whole gate ran. 52 were caught and four were not.

The four were real holes rather than curiosities. The worst was in the browser code: the compound-to-arithmetic conversion could be inverted there and every check still passed, which would have corrupted any expected return a reader typed in and every point of the sensitivity exhibit, while the same defect in the Python was caught at once. The others were the trading year, which then fed the volatility and could be set to 200 days undetected, at a cost of about half the recommended share; the dividend yield, which could be taken from a stale month; and three fitted-grid constants that nothing read at all. All four are closed, and re-running the sweep then killed all 56, leaving only a control mutant that must survive or the exercise would prove nothing. The statistics, trading year included, have since been deleted, because neither variant measures its volatility any more.

What this does not establish. A mutation sweep measures whether the checks bind the code as written. It cannot tell you the code is the right model, and none of the 56 defects touched the two things most likely to be wrong here, which are the expected return and the assumption that returns do not repeat themselves. Those are argued in sections 3.1 and 2.4, and they are arguments rather than measurements.

8.4 Whether the answer discriminates

A recommendation that comes out at 100% for everyone is not advice. Across a grid of 1,040 single-adult households, every combination of ages 25 to 85 in steps of five, wages of $40,000, $80,000, $150,000 and $250,000, savings of $25,000, $100,000, $500,000, $1,500,000 and $3,000,000, and risk aversion of 2, 4, 6 and 8, 33.3% of answers are pinned at 100% and none at 0%, leaving about two in three interior. Adults of 67 or more are treated as Choi's spreadsheet treats them, retiring after this year on 40% of today's wage, here up to the ceiling of section 7.7. With the double-counted buyback estimate of section 3.1, 43.8% would be pinned. The saturation is concentrated where it should be, among the young, whose human capital dwarfs their savings, and where the model's own honest answer is that the constraint against borrowing binds. A frequency across a grid is a fact about the grid, so the useful form is the threshold, which depends only on savings as a multiple of the wage while the ceiling of section 7.7 does not bind, because human capital is then proportional to the wage. At the default risk aversion, the cap binds below 2.7 years of salary saved at age 25, 1.8 at 35, 1.4 at 45, 1.3 at 55 and 1.0 at 65, and not above. Where the ceiling binds the threshold is lower, most near retirement: 0.7 years at 65 on $150,000. Anyone under those levels is reading the constraint at work rather than the formula's own figure, which is why the page prints what the formula asks for whenever the cap binds: across the pinned answers of that grid the median asks for 2.9 times the portfolio, and the extreme corner, a 25-year-old on $250,000 with $25,000 saved and a risk aversion of 2, for 81 times. Ayres and Nalebuff (2010) take that result at face value and argue a young household should borrow to reach it. This tool does not follow them, because the model it approximates does not allow borrowing: the clip is Cocco, Gomes and Maenhout's no-borrowing constraint, which Choi's equation (9) carries, and for a household that cannot borrow the model's answer is 100%. The larger figure is what the formula alone would ask for.

8.5 What has not been checked, in order of what it would cost

Uncertainty in the expected return. The model treats a point estimate as known. Section 3.1 shows that estimate spanning 7.07% to 2.42% across methods that are all defensible, a range worth the entire allocation. Barberis (2000) shows that admitting the uncertainty lowers the equity share, and by more at longer horizons, which is the reverse of the usual intuition. This is the largest gap in the model and nothing here compensates for it.

The permanent wage shock volatility. Section 5.2 measures it at 22 points of the answer across its plausible range, second only to the expected return, and it is a constant the user cannot see or change. Validating it would mean re-estimating earnings risk on current data rather than inheriting a 2005 calibration, and ideally letting a household say whether its own earnings are more secure than average.

Anything outside the United States. Every input, and the earnings risk calibration behind the discount rates, is United States data. Section 3.5 explains why a global version is not offered rather than offered badly, and the page says at the top that it is for the United States only.

The buyback adjustment, which was measured and rejected. AQR corrects its historical growth rate by the change in buyback intensity: the 10-year net buyback yield minus the 25-year one, on the reasoning that a company retiring shares faster than it used to will grow earnings per share faster than its own history suggests. Doing the same here needs a net buyback yield reaching back as far as the growth window. Building it from Damodaran's payout yield less the dividend yield gives 173 months, from January 2012, which is already inside the buyback era; over that window the 10-year average is 2.37% against a full-sample 2.54%, so the adjustment comes out at minus 0.18 points. That is the opposite sign to the effect it exists to capture, and it is an artefact of a window too short to contain the change it is trying to measure. AQR's own term also travels with a cross-sectional shrink and a growth adjustment that partly offset it, so taking one piece of their construction without the others would not be doing what they do. The gap this leaves is half a point, named in section 8.3, and a documented gap is better than a correction with the wrong sign.

The estimator in a buyback regime. The backtest in section 3.1 runs from 1910, and buybacks only became a material share of payout after 1982. Both terms of equation (5) are measured on a century in which companies issued shares on net, and the test cannot say how the estimator behaves in the regime the tool is now being used in. This is a known limit with a known direction, upward, and no way to size it from this data.

The house, and the mortgage on it. Choi's guide puts both outside: "enter into the investable net worth cell the value of non-housing assets minus the value of non-mortgage debt". Existing shares and bonds are inside it, so a household that already holds equities is not a difficulty at all; the answer is a target for the whole pot, to be compared with what is held now. A house is different, because it is a large, undiversified, usually levered holding of a risky asset that the model cannot see.

Counting home equity in total wealth and subtracting whatever equity exposure the house already supplies, which is how human capital is treated one section up, gives a range rather than a number: for a household with $500,000 invested, a $600,000 house and a $200,000 mortgage, anywhere from 47.2% if a house is a pure bond to 0% if it is as risky as equities, against the 37.3% the tool reports by ignoring it. Housing volatility is usually put at 10% to 15% against equity's 18.5%, with a low correlation. The house's $400,000 of equity then counts as equity exposure in proportion to the correlation times the ratio of the two volatilities, and going from 10% volatility and a correlation of 0.2 to 15% and 0.4 narrows the range to 38.6% down to 21.3%. The mortgage is worth 17 points on its own in the other direction, since a mortgage is a negative bond and netting $200,000 of it against investable wealth moves the answer to 53.9%.

The theory does not settle it either. Grossman and Laroque (1990) treat the house as a durable with transaction costs, never rebalanced. Flavin and Yamashita (2002) treat it as a levered undiversified holding that crowds equities out. Cocco (2005) solves the very model this tool approximates with housing added and finds the same crowding out for the young and the poor. Against all three, Yao and Zhang (2005) find homeowners hold a lower equity share of net worth but a higher share of liquid assets, because owning hedges rent and so substitutes for the safe asset.

So housing is left out rather than approximated, and this section exists to say what that costs rather than to plan a fix. What survives all four papers is one sentence: a household whose house is large relative to its portfolio, and still mortgaged, should read this tool's answer as an upper bound.

Higher moments. Equation (3) keeps only the mean and the variance, so skewness and fat tails are not in the model at all. Including them means estimating co-skewness and co-kurtosis, which are noisy at the sample sizes available, for what the literature suggests is a small shift in the recommendation. It is a real omission and it is not measured here.

Whether anyone acts on it. No claim is made that a household following this allocation is better off than one following a simpler rule, only that this allocation is the one the model implies. Section 6 reports what the alternative rules cost inside the model, which is a different and much weaker statement.

8.6 Retirees, where several errors meet

A retired household is where most of the model's simplifications meet, and they do not all point the same way. Each is stated here with its direction. None is corrected. The comparisons with the full model below are from a direct solution of the full Cocco, Gomes and Maenhout model run for this project (not published), on the data of 3 September 2026 and Choi's 18.5% volatility, for a single retiree living on a pension.

Where the answer comes down from 100%: the formula runs high. At a risk aversion of 5 that happens between about one and three and a half years of pension saved. At two and a half years the formula is 8 to 12 points above the full model for retirees of 67 to 85, at three years 5 to 7, at four up to 2, and just below 100% up to 25: at 85 with a year and a half saved, 98.9% against 74%. The band is not fixed. At a risk aversion of 3 it sits further out, from about two to six years, where the formula is up to 18 points high; at 8 it sits further in, at one to two years, up to 22. It moves with the market too: on the inputs of 29 September 2026, with the safe rate at 3.32%, the formula is 6 to 8 points high at two and a half years from 67 to 80, against 8 to 12 on 3 September. No number of years of pension marks it, which is one reason the page no longer prints one.

The 30-year safe rate: the answer runs low. For a household living off its savings, the matching riskless asset is not a 30-year bond but a ladder of inflation-linked bonds paying out to about age 100. Weighting each year by the chance of being alive in it, from the 2019 NCHS life table, that ladder has a duration of about 10 years at 67, 7.5 at 75 and 5 at 85, so its yield sits near the 10-year real rate or below it. On 3 September 2026 the 10-year TIPS yield was 2.43% and the 5-year 2.16%, both annualised, against the 2.98% the tool uses (Table 13). Read at the 10-year rate, a single retiree of 67 to 85 with 10 to 30 years of pension saved gets 4 to 10 points more equity, and 11 to 17 with five years.

Couples: the answer runs high. The model values each adult's pension as Choi's one-person model does and adds the two, so both count in full in every future year. In fact one of them stops when its owner dies, and Social Security then pays the survivor the larger of the two benefits. Weighting each pension by the chance that its owner is alive, given that at least one of the two is, from the same life table, and paying the survivor the larger one, the pension wealth of couples aged 62 to 80, the second up to three years younger, with pensions of $15,000 to $40,000 each, comes out 10 to 30% lower: least for a couple of 67 and 64 on $40,000 and $15,000, most for a couple of 80 on equal pensions. The part of the answer above the Merton share is proportional to that wealth, so it is too high by about the same proportion: 1 to 5 points for those couples with 17 to 38 years of the larger pension saved, and 2 to 18 with 5 to 10 years. Choi's spreadsheet adds the two adults the same way.

Setting money aside: two versions of the advice were wrong. Earlier versions of the page's heading and of section 1.1 told readers to type in only money with no planned use, or that they would leave alone through a bad decade, which for a retiree meant taking years of spending out first and left the household with too little in equities overall. A later version told retirees to leave out this year's spending, which takes out a year of spending without counting the year of pension that pays for it, and so ran high: by 9.5 points for a retiree of 70 with five years of a $40,000 pension saved, and by up to 35 with less. Section 1.1 and the hint under the savings field now follow the paper's accounting: a retiree leaves out only the part of this year's spending the pension does not cover, and money for a dated goal.

Together. Measured together, with the full model solved at the 10-year rate the ladder's duration points to and the tool at its own 30-year rate, the safe rate wins nearly everywhere. At a risk aversion of 5 the tool is 4 to 15 points below the full model from two and a half years of pension saved upwards. Below that both are at or near 100% and within a point of each other, apart from retirees of 85 and over, where the tool is up to 6 points above or 9 below. At a risk aversion of 8 the tool is 4 to 15 points below from a year and a half saved; at 3 it is up to 16 below once its answer leaves 100%. So in the corner the two errors largely cancel, and elsewhere a single retiree's answer runs low. For couples the survivor rule pulls the other way: for the couples above with 17 to 27 years saved, reading at the 10-year rate adds about 6 to 11 points, against the 1.4 to 5.1 the survivor rule takes away, and with little saved the two are of similar size. No direction holds for every retiree, so the page prints none. The hint that told retirees with savings under two years of pension to read the answer as an upper bound is gone. Measured at a retiree's matching safe rate, the answers it covered were mostly within a point of the full model at a risk aversion of 5 and mostly 4 to 11 points below it at 8, so the direction it gave was at best idle and often wrong.
