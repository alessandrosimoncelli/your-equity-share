Your Equity Share, Italy

What the Italian variant does differently, and what checks each number

Version 1.0, September 2026. Companion to the technical methodology, which describes the model itself. This document does not restate Merton (1969), Choi, Liu and Liu (2025), the human capital layer or the derivation of the equity share. Those are shared between the two variants and are argued there.

Every market figure here is as of 8 September 2026 and will have moved. The Python tool and the Italian page recompute them from the market data they ship with, refreshed every Monday, except the volatility, which is fixed (section 6).

1. What this document is

One model, two countries. src/your_equity_share is shared and is not duplicated, so both variants take their dividend yield from their own index, net of withholding tax at source where a holder suffers any (MSCI's net index for the world fund, none for an American holding American stock), both take real growth in earnings per share from the same hundred-year trend through Shiller, both assume no repricing, and a test asserts the growth term is identical to five decimal places rather than merely close. It runs wherever Shiller's workbook is present, which includes every Monday refresh that succeeds; the workbook is not in the repository, so a plain push skips it.

That is deliberate and it is the point of the whole arrangement. The gap between a 20.7% American answer and a 64.6% Italian one is the country and not the method. That is the reference household used throughout: 45 years old, 100,000 a year after tax, 1,500,000 saved, risk aversion 5. Neither variant applies tax, which Choi's model does not contain, and for Italy section 8 measures why that is right and why the one tax rule in Choi's guide is not applied either. Were the model copied into two folders, the copies would be edited on different days and every later comparison would quietly mix a country difference with a code difference.

The page follows the same rule. The Italian page beside this document is not a copy of the American one: the build writes it from the same source, in Italian, with this variant's calibration and its own market file, so a fix to the page reaches both.

Every number below is checked against something outside this project, and section 2 is that list in one table. Where a figure cannot be checked, section 11 says so rather than dressing it up.

3. The equity sleeve

Global, not Italian. An Italian investor buying a home index would hold about 0.8% of world market capitalisation, which is Italy's weight in MSCI World. The implementation assumed is FTSE All-World through an accumulating UCITS share class, which is what most European retail investors actually hold.

The check is that this is the standard finding rather than a preference. Home bias is one of the most documented anomalies in international finance: French and Poterba (1991) measured investors holding overwhelmingly domestic portfolios against what diversification implies, and Coeurdacier and Rey (2013) survey three decades of work finding the same thing persists. No part of the literature recommends concentrating a lifetime portfolio in a market that is 0.8% of world capitalisation.

The consequence for this document is that the American variant's inputs cannot simply be reused. A different index has a different yield, a different volatility and a different currency, and each is measured below.

4. The expected return

E[Rreal] = dividend yield + real growth in earnings per share + repricing
(5)

The same three terms the American variant uses, which is what makes the two answers comparable. Today: 1.1917% + 2.2860% + 0 = 3.4777% compound, which is 4.4947% as an arithmetic mean at this volatility. The yield is net of the withholding tax the fund suffers at source; gross it is 1.5339% (section 4.1).

4.1 The dividend yield

Measured, from MSCI's own index levels rather than from anyone's forecast. The gross index reinvests dividends and the price index does not, so the gap between their returns over a month is that month's dividend, and twelve of those over the price at the end are a trailing yield.

Over the price at the end, and the denominator is worth a third of a point. Three constructions all sound like a trailing twelve-month yield and this year they disagree badly, because the index rose 22% across the window. Measured gross: dividends over the price a year ago give 1.87%, each month's dividend over that month's own starting price gives 1.70%, and dividends over today's price give 1.53%. Shiller's dividend column is a trailing annual rate divided by his last price, so the third is the object the two variants must share.

The check. AQR quote 1.6% for this index, gross. The gross measurement gives 1.5339%, 0.07 points apart. Correcting the denominator improved that agreement rather than worsening it, which is the sign that the correction was right.

Net of withholding tax, 1.1917%, and that is the figure used. The country a company is based in taxes the dividend it pays abroad before the money leaves, so a world fund receives its dividends already reduced. MSCI's net index reinvests them after that withholding, and over the same twelve months it takes 0.3422 points off the gross yield. No holder of the fund ever receives the money withheld: VWCE's net asset value is already net of what the fund pays, which is a little less than MSCI deducts (next paragraph). It is a property of the fund, like its price, and not the household's own tax, which the model leaves out (section 8). An American holding American stock suffers none, which is why the American variant's gross Shiller column is the same measurement there. Until October 2026 this variant used the gross yield and recorded the withholding beside it, which credited the household with dividends the fund never receives.

The net index is the right input because the fund delivers it, not because its withholding rates are the fund's. MSCI deducts withholding at the rate a non-resident institution pays without the benefit of a tax treaty, the most a fund can suffer. A fund domiciled where treaties apply pays less: VWCE, in Ireland, pays 15% on American dividends rather than 30%. It also pays its own costs, and the two roughly cancel. Over the five, six and seven years to August 2026, VWCE's price on Xetra rose 0.06 to 0.07 points a year less than MSCI ACWI in euro net, and about 0.58 points a year less than the gross index. VWCE tracks FTSE All-World rather than MSCI ACWI, so part of that small gap is the difference between the two indices. Measured for this revision on the fund's daily closes and MSCI's month-end levels, which are not kept in the repository.

4.2 The growth term

The American variant's own hundred-year ordinary least squares trend through Shiller's real earnings per share, used unchanged as the global rate. This is the largest judgement in the variant and it has three checks, one of which is a measurement of what it costs.

AQR publish both halves, which is the only direct evidence anyone can offer: 2.7% real EPS growth for United States large cap against 2.6% for Global All Country. A tenth of a point apart.

Theory expects that. Long-run real growth in earnings per share is a return on retained capital net of share issuance, not a national characteristic, and nothing in a border makes retained euros compound differently from retained dollars over a lifetime.

And the alternative was tried and failed. tools/global_growth.py rebuilds global real dividend growth from the Jorda-Schularick-Taylor Macrohistory Database, eighteen advanced economies from 1870. The answer depends on the weighting scheme far more than on the data: weights pinned at today give 0.89% to 1.52%, pinned at the start of the window 1.86% to 3.04%, GDP shares each year 2.77% to 5.43%. An envelope 4.5 points wide against 0.25 points for the American estimator. The cause is structural: a fixed basket of countries is not an index, because an index reconstitutes and JST publishes country index returns rather than the world index's constituents.

What the substitution costs, measured. tools/us_vs_global.py puts the question to ex-US data, because comparing the S&P 500 with a world index is largely comparing the United States with itself.

The global index is about 64% American, so an American growth rate is only wrong on the other 36%, and a gap of d points costs 0.36d on the global rate.

The direction is settled and the size is not. Every estimate not built on a biased construction says the United States grew faster, so this growth term is too high, by something between a tenth and half a point. AQR's figure is a lower bound rather than a best guess, because they shrink cross-country growth differences 50% toward the global average by construction.

It is not corrected, and that is a decision rather than an oversight. Scaling by AQR's ratio looks principled and is not: they publish to one decimal place, and propagating that rounding through the 64% weight puts the implied gap anywhere between 0.10 and 0.59 points. An adjustment less reliable than the thing it adjusts is not an improvement. One consolation is real: measured gross, as AQR measure theirs, the estimate this produces is already 0.38 points below their published figure for the same index, so even at the top of the bias range it lands under the number the firms publish.

4.3 Why not AQR's own figure

This variant took AQR's 4.20% whole until September 2026. What ended that is their own sentence about it:

We present local real (inflation-adjusted) and nominal annual compound rates of return for a horizon of 5 to 10 years. Over such intermediate horizons, starting valuations tend to be useful inputs. For multi-decade forecast horizons their impact is diluted, so theory and long-term historical averages may matter more in judging expected returns.

Both halves of their number are built to ten years, not to thirty. The payout half uses "a country-specific estimate of next-10-year real EPS growth". The earnings half multiplies the cyclically adjusted earnings yield by 1 + (g x 5) explicitly "to account for earnings growth during the 10-year window".

A current yield plus a long-run growth rate with no repricing carries no horizon at all, because nothing in it forecasts when anything happens. It is the same number at ten years or at thirty. And Table 7 of the American methodology shows it is also the one with the lowest error at thirty years, 2.25 root mean square error against 3.31 for the trailing earnings yield, which wins at ten and twenty and loses at thirty.

The mismatch was conservative, which is why it survived so long without looking like a problem. Horizon Actuarial, who survey about forty advisors and split the answers into ten-year and twenty-year horizons, report that returns "are expected to be lower in the short term compared to the long term", with differences "relatively large for equities". Vanguard, the one firm publishing both horizons off a single model run, has its thirty-year United States forecast 0.9 points above its ten-year.

AQR's two halves are entered by hand from their annual report and compared on every refresh. If either drifts more than a third of a point from ours, the refresh prints a warning; it does not stop the refresh.

4.4 Is the pairing sound

This variant pairs a global dividend yield with an American growth trend, and payout policy trades one against the other: a market that pays more of its earnings as dividends has a higher yield and correspondingly lower per-share growth. Mixing them would count the difference twice. It is the buyback double-count of the American document's section 3.1, one market over instead of one term over.

The earnings yield settles it, because E/P does not care how earnings are split between dividends, buybacks and retention. iShares, same provider and same methodology on both sides, 8 September 2026. The global earnings yield is taken net of the same 0.342 points of withholding as the estimate, so the two compare like with like; an American holder suffers none, so nothing comes off the American side:

The concern is real and it is small, and it runs the other way. Global does pay out more of its earnings as dividends, 36.5% against 33.3%, so part of its yield advantage is policy rather than value: splitting the 0.340 point gap between the two funds' quoted yields, 1.43% and 1.09%, gives 0.212 to valuation and 0.128 to payout policy. But on the payout-neutral measure, net of withholding, global offers 0.296 points more than the United States and this estimate credits it only 0.095, so the construction understates the global advantage by 0.20 points rather than inflating it. Before withholding the two gaps are 0.638 and 0.437, and the difference is the same 0.20, because the same 0.342 comes off both global figures. Each variant also lands within about a tenth of a point of its own market's earnings yield, which is what soundness looks like: equation (5) and E/P coincide when retained earnings earn the cost of equity.

5. The safe asset

The ECB's AAA euro area government curve at thirty years, less the market break-even inflation rate of the longest euro linker. 1.5447%. Under the answer the page names the asset the rest of the savings goes to: long-dated inflation-linked government bonds, at the rate in its Exhibit 5. That rate is the AAA curve's, so the bonds meant are AAA issuers' linkers such as the Bund/euro-i, not BTP (below).

It took three attempts and each failed a different test, which is worth recording because each looked right at the time.

Two conventions are matched before anything is subtracted. The ECB publishes its curve continuously compounded. Its technical notes say so: "The continuous method is used to compound interest rates." The model reads annual rates, so the thirty-year spot of 3.7617% is first annualised, to 3.8333%. The Deutsche Finanzagentur defines its break-even as a simple yield difference between the linker and a conventional Bund, so it comes off by subtraction rather than being divided out: 3.8333% less 2.2886% is 1.5447%. Until October 2026 this variant read the continuous rate as an annual one and divided the break-even out, which gave 1.4401%, about a tenth of a point low and worth 3.1 points of equity share for the reference household.

Constant maturity is the property that matters, and it is the property FRED's DFII30 has. DFII30 is not a bond: it is the thirty-year point read off the TIPS curve every day, so it describes the same horizon at every refresh. The 2046 has 19.6 years left and one fewer every year, so within a decade it would have been quoting a fifteen-year horizon under a thirty-year label.

A break-even is a price, not a forecast. It is the rate at which holding the linker and holding a nominal bond pay the same. The Survey of Professional Forecasters is an opinion, and deflating by it puts an opinion inside a market rate. Divided out, the way a forecast of inflation is, it would give 1.7606% today, 0.22 points above the rate used and above the 2046's traded real yield, and it would lower the reference household's answer by 6.1 points. On another day the survey could sit above the break-even and the error would run the other way; the reason to refuse it is the same either way.

The construction checks against itself, and comes close. The break-even is derived from the 2046, so subtracting it from a nominal yield should land near that bond's own traded real yield of 1.4906%. It gives 1.5447%, about five hundredths of a point above. Because the break-even is a conventional Bund's yield less the 2046's real yield, what is left is the gap between the curve's thirty-year point and that Bund's yield, and that gap is not one thing: a thirty-year nominal leg against a 19.6-year inflation leg, a composite of AAA issuers against Germany alone, and a zero-coupon rate against a coupon bond's yield. Nothing here separates the three, and nothing is fitted to close the gap. The check is external: the 1.4906% is the issuer's own published figure, from the Deutsche Finanzagentur, not a number this project computed.

AAA rather than every euro area government, which yields 0.64 points more at the same maturity. That spread is compensation for a government not paying; equation (4) cannot represent default risk, and booking a credit premium as a risk-free return would lower the recommendation while looking prudent. An Italian household buying BTP is not holding this asset.

Two honest weaknesses. The curve is a composite of all AAA-rated euro area governments rather than one issuer, and its membership changes with ratings, so it is not a constant object across time. And it tracks euro area HICP rather than Italian: only BTP Italia follows Italian inflation, at five years and retail.

6. Volatility

13.9860%, fixed. The annualised standard deviation of monthly log returns of MSCI All Country World in euro, gross, from December 2000 to August 2026. That is the convention Choi, Liu and Liu use for their 18.5%, monthly CRSP value-weighted log returns from July 1926 to July 2024; theirs are in excess of the one-month Treasury bill and this one is on total rather than excess returns. Like theirs it is one number for a whole life, which is how they and Cocco, Gomes and Maenhout treat volatility. It is priced in euro, so it already carries the currency risk an unhedged global fund gives a euro investor without anything further being done to it. It is written into the code once and changed only by hand.

Why fixed. Until October 2026 it was measured again every Monday from five years of the fund's daily closes, and a trailing window turns volatility into something that jumps when a crash enters or leaves it. In February and March 2025 the crash of February and March 2020 left the window: the five-year figure fell from 16.7% to 14.5% in five weeks, with no change in long-run risk. On this document's market figures that alone moves the answer for an uncapped household (45 years old, 30,000 euro of pay, 500,000 saved, risk 5) by nine points, from 49% to 58%, and on the figures in use before this revision it moved it by more.

It looks low against published assumptions, and much of the difference is the currency. Horizon Actuarial's 2024 survey of 41 advisors gives 16.5% for United States large cap and 18.1% for non-US developed, both in dollars. That gap needed explaining before this figure could be used, so it was measured on one index over one window:

Two things follow and both are measurements. The currency is worth 1.73 points on the same index over the same window, because the dollar tends to rise when equities fall, so for a euro holder the currency gain offsets part of the equity loss. And the long window is the one to use: twenty and twenty-five years give 13.37% and 13.99%, while the last five, which were calm, give 12.92%, a point lower.

A correction. An earlier version of this section said the five-year daily figure then in use, 13.8942%, reproduced the twenty-five-year monthly one to a tenth of a point. It did not confirm anything: two gaps of opposite sign largely cancelled. The calm window takes 1.07 points off the twenty-five-year figure (Table 5), and on the fund over those same five years daily returns give about 1.3 points more than monthly ones. Most of that second gap is the fund's own closing prices in Frankfurt rather than daily sampling as such: on the index over the same five years daily returns add only 0.2 to 0.4 points. Neither gap says anything about long-run risk. How much rests on the level itself, and why it is a judgement rather than a measurement anyone can check, is in section 11.

7. The career and the pension

The household is an Italian private-sector employee. Valuing future earnings takes three things: how pay moves with age, how much it can move by surprise, and the pension at the end. All three are Italian estimates of that one person, set in ITALY_CALIBRATION rather than in the market data because they belong to the household and the state, not to the market. With the American graduate's career and the OECD's 79%, the reference household of section 1 held 55.1%; with these, 64.6%. More bond-like wealth leaves room for more equity in the financial portfolio, the direction Bodie, Merton and Samuelson (1992) predict.

The TFR is in neither estimate, so the page asks for it. Under article 2120 of the codice civile the employer sets aside each year the year's pay divided by 13.5, which is 6.91% of gross pay once the 0.5% contribution of law 297 of 1982 comes off it, revalues it every year at 1.5% plus three quarters of inflation, and pays it when the job ends, when it is taxed separately (TUIR, articles 17 and 19). Daminato and Padula measure the wages received in each year and the Treasury's 66% is the public pension alone, so both leave it out. The page asks for it twice: the year's accrual after its tax goes in the wage, about 7.5% of net pay, and what has already accrued goes in savings, counted at about three quarters for the tax still to come. TFR left with the employer is part of the safe share, so the page says to apply the percentage to the total and put all the equity in the rest. This is the rule of Choi, Liu and Liu's guide for an employer's retirement contributions, which go in the wage, applied to Italy.

7.1 The career

From Daminato and Padula (2024), in the Journal of the European Economic Association: the earnings process of Italian private-sector employees, estimated on the Bank of Italy's Survey on Household Income and Wealth from 1986 to 2008, for a life-cycle model of saving and portfolio choice of the same family as Choi's. They estimate it in Cocco, Gomes and Maenhout's own form, a cubic in age plus permanent and transitory shocks, which is the form this model takes. Earnings are after income tax, the definition of Jappelli and Pistaferri (2010), the same basis as the after-tax wage the page asks for.

Why it fits this model. The permanent shock enters Choi's discount rate as a regressor, and 12.3% sits inside the 10.2% to 13.0% he fitted over. The temporary shock is below his range, but its coefficient is 0.028 and it moves the rate by a tenth of a point. The age profile is not a regressor at all: it only sets the wages being discounted, and Choi's own worked example, section 3.3 of the paper, uses an earnings path outside his fitted set and lands within 3 points of the full solution.

The checks. Daminato and Padula report that their permanent variance falls inside the confidence interval of Jappelli and Pistaferri (2010), estimated on the same survey with the same definition of earnings.

The age profile is the weaker part. Their age coefficients are imprecise one by one, and the cube is printed to one significant digit, −0.000006. Checked against the INPS Osservatorio on private employees for 2024, which counts contributions rather than surveying households, it rises about twice as fast: +19.7% from 47 to 62 on their profile, against +10.2% in men's daily gross pay from 45-49 to 60-64. The two are not the same object, since INPS is one year's cross-section of gross pay rather than one generation's pay after tax, but both rise where the American graduate's pay falls. So the direction is settled and the slope is less certain. It matters most to the young: at risk 5 a profile half as steep lowers the answer by up to about 10 points at 25 and 4 points at 49, the most for savings near where the answer leaves 100%, about 7 to 8 times pay, and less above that. Below about 6.7 times pay the answer is 100% either way.

Whom it describes. Married household heads employed in the private or public sector, aged about 25 to 60, of every education level; this variant uses the private-sector estimates, the larger group. The self-employed, whose income is riskier, are outside it, and so is any split by education.

7.2 The pension

66%, the Italian Treasury's projected net replacement rate for a private employee who retires in 2050 at 66 years and 2 months with 38 years of contributions: Ragioneria Generale dello Stato, Le tendenze di medio-lungo periodo del sistema pensionistico e socio-sanitario, Rapporto n. 26 (2025), Table 6.3.a, base case. Net means after tax and contributions, the same basis as the after-tax wage the page asks for.

Why that row. The model retires everyone at 67, because Choi fitted his coefficients there and the age cannot be moved from outside them, and 2050 is within two years of when today's 45-year-old reaches 67. The same table gives 63% to 64% for its other cases near 67 and 73% for an old-age retirement at 69, so 66% sits where a retirement at 67 falls.

Whom it understates. The same row of Table 6.3.a gives 77.0% for a retirement in 2030 and 67.4% for one in 2040. The model uses 66% for everyone, so it understates the pension of anyone retiring before about 2040. For an uncapped household at risk 5 that understates the answer by up to about 8 points for a retirement in 2030, falling to under a point by 2040. Someone within ten years of retirement can do better than any table, and the page's pension hint says so: INPS's own estimate, "La mia pensione futura", converted to net, is more precise than 66% and goes in the year-by-year box, a year of it after tax in each year it will be paid.

Whom it overstates. The model puts no ceiling on the pension. For workers first insured from 1996, or who chose the contributory method, INPS counts pay only up to 122,295 euro of gross pay in 2026 (law 335 of 1995, article 2, paragraph 18; INPS circular 6 of 30 January 2026, section 6), so for someone paid more than that the model overstates the pension, and with it the equity share.

Why not the OECD's 79%. This variant used it until October 2026: the future net rate for an average earner in Pensions at a Glance 2025, Italy country note. The figure is right, but it describes a worker who contributes for 48 years, from 22 to the future normal retirement age of 70. Italian pensions are contributory: the pension is the contributions paid, revalued, times a conversion coefficient that rises with the age at retirement. Retiring at 67 means fewer contributions and a smaller coefficient, so paying 79% from 67 overstated the pension.

What the page asks for. The pension field is a pension already paid to you, a year of it after tax, even while you still work: an old-age, survivor's or disability pension. It is paid every year from now and, since it does not depend on working, is discounted at the benefit rates from today. The pension the current job will pay is not typed: it is imputed at 66% of the final wage and added when the wage stops. The exception is someone of 66 or more still earning: the career pension is 66% of today's wage, and if a pension is already being paid it is taken to be that career's and nothing is added. A partner has their own field for a pension already paid to them, and a pension of either that starts later goes in that person's year-by-year box. Every year is entered as Choi, Liu and Liu's guide asks, "assuming you are still alive in each future year": the calculations already allow for the chance of dying, at United States rates (section 11). The box follows the age, wage and pension fields until a year in it is edited; from then on a note under it says the typed years replace those fields, and a reset brings the projection back.

7.3 Retirees

A retired household's answer carries four known errors, and a fifth the page no longer invites. They point different ways and partly offset, and none is corrected.

Little saved: too much equity. In the United States a direct solution of the full Cocco, Gomes and Maenhout model run for this project (not published) finds the formula 10 to 20 points too high for retirees whose savings are about two years of pension or less (6 to 7 points at two and a half), and the American page tells a retiree with under about two years of pension saved to read the answer as an upper bound. Choi's retirement discount rates are the same in this variant, but the corner has not been measured on Italian inputs, and the Italian page does not carry that line.

The thirty-year rate: too little equity. The riskless asset that matches a retiree's spending is a ladder of inflation-linked bonds running to about 100. With spending that falls with age, as it does in Cocco, Gomes and Maenhout's model, its duration is about 14 years at 67, 11 at 75 and 7 at 85, and the euro real curve sits lower there than at thirty years. On this document's date the construction of section 5, with the break-even read between the 2033 and 2046 linkers, gives 1.21% at ten years against 1.5447% at thirty, and the Finanzagentur's 2033 linker yields 0.99% real against 1.49% for the 2046. At the rate of each age's ladder, the retired households with a pension that were tried would hold about 3 to 6 points more equity between 67 and 75, about 7 more at 80 and 9 at 85. The American methodology (section 8.6) weights each year by the chance of being alive in it instead. On the euro curve that gives about 11 years at 67, 8 at 75 and 5 at 85, and about 7 to 10 points more equity between 67 and 75, 10 at 80 and 13 at 85. The two weightings bracket the effect.

Couples: too much equity. The model counts both pensions for as long as either spouse lives, and Choi's spreadsheet sums the two adults the same way. Under Italian rules a surviving spouse keeps their own pension and 60% of the other's, the reversibilità, and less when their own income is high. With that rule at the full 60%, a retired couple's pension wealth is about 8 to 10% lower than the model counts, and lower still where the survivor's own income cuts the 60%, so the part of the answer above the no-pension share, 29.3% at risk 5, is too high by about that proportion: 1.4 to 5.5 points in the couples tried, the most where savings are small against the pensions.

Tax while spending down: too much equity. Section 8 holds the money to one date. A retiree sells some of both funds every year, so part of the money is taxed after only a few years, where tax lowers the right share most. A simulation run for this revision on this document's snapshot, and not kept in the repository, of retirees aged 67 to 75 who spend their savings over 14 to 20 years and rebalance every year puts the right share 5% to 11% below the model's answer: about 3 to 6 points too much equity.

Money set aside: no longer invited. The page used to describe its money as having "no planned use or withdrawal date", which invited a retiree to keep years of spending out of the savings typed in. That money still sits somewhere, usually in cash, so the household ended up with less equity than the model wants for its whole wealth. The page now speaks of money meant to last the rest of your life, with no fixed date to spend it, and its savings hint tells a retiree to include what they will draw down over the years and leave out only this year's spending and money for a dated goal. That is Choi, Liu and Liu's own accounting: savings are what is left after this year's consumption (equations 5 to 7 and section 1.3 of their paper), and the drawing down in later years is the model's own consumption.

8. Tax

The Italian answer reads the market figures as they are, and this section says why. Choi, Liu and Liu do not model tax: their variables are "implicitly after-tax where relevant". The guide to their spreadsheet has one tax rule, to cut the safe rate by the marginal tax rate when bonds are held in a taxable account. For an American saving in a 401(k) or an IRA the market figures are close to after-tax ones and the rule does not apply. For Italy the rule is not applied, because it does not describe Italian law. The law taxes both funds, not only the bonds, and at sale rather than every year: 26% on the gain of an equity fund, 12.5% on a fund of government bonds of Italy and white-list states, in proportion to what it holds in them (Agenzia delle Entrate, Circolare 19/E of 2014, section 5), and 0.2% a year of imposta di bollo on the value of each. Both rates fall on the nominal gain and only when units are sold, death counts as a sale (Circolare 19/E of 2013, section 2.4), and a loss on one fund cannot be set against a gain on another.

The guide's rule would move the answer the wrong way. Cutting only the safe rate, by the 12.5% a government bond fund pays, takes it from 1.5447% to 1.3516% and raises the answer. For an uncapped household aged 45 to 65 at risk 5 it adds up to about 8 or 9 points just above the savings where the answer leaves 100%, and less as savings grow, about 3 points at fifty times pay (5.8 for the reference household), and slightly more for younger households. Taxing the equity fund as well, which is what the law does, lowers it, as the rest of this section measures.

Tax takes a share of the risk as well as of the return. A fund held for many years is usually in gain when it is sold, 85% of the time at seven years and 98% at thirty on this document's snapshot (Table 7), so the state takes about a quarter of the gain whether markets did well or badly: it shares the swings, not only the average. That is the effect Domar and Musgrave (1944) described. Taking tax off the expected return and leaving the volatility as it is counts the first half and misses the second, and this variant did exactly that until October 2026.

Measured directly. tools/tax_check.py takes the two funds through the law as written: the equity fund's price is uncertain, 26% of its nominal gain is paid on sale with no credit for a loss, 12.5% of the bond fund's, and the stamp duty every year. It then finds the equity share a household with risk aversion 5 should hold until a given date, with the tax and without it, exactly rather than by simulation.

So the model reads the market figures as they are. The tax lowers the right equity share by about a quarter for money sold at three years, by about an eighth at seven years and by under a tenth at twelve years and beyond, and a household that ignores it gives up under 2 basis points a year of return at three years and under half a basis point from seven years on. Taking tax off the expected return alone cut the share by 17% to 32%. At three years the two are close, 0.682 of the untaxed share against the law's 0.745, so there taxing the mean is nearer the law than leaving tax out. From seven years on it cuts two to four times what the law does, and there leaving tax out is both the simpler reading and the closer one.

The missing loss credit matters less than it looks. Losses on funds cannot offset gains, which is why this document once argued that the state shares only the upside. It does share only the upside, but after three years a fund is below cost when sold about one time in four, after seven about one time in seven, and after twelve under one time in ten, so the upside is most of what there is for money held long.

9. Currency

Three of the four inputs are euro figures by construction. The dividend yield is measured on a euro-priced global index, the volatility on a euro-priced series, and the safe rate is a euro curve less a euro break-even.

The growth term is the exception, and it is one term of three. It is real growth in United States earnings per share, used as the global real rate. Reading a dollar real growth rate as a euro one needs the assumption AQR state for exactly this purpose: "one simple assumption is that relative purchasing power is preserved, which implies the expected spot currency return is equal to the expected inflation differential. Under this assumption, expected real returns are the same regardless of currency."

The literature says when that is safe and when it is not, and the answer is a matter of horizon. Rogoff (1996) describes a "remarkable consensus" of three to five year half-lives for deviations from purchasing power parity, which is about 13 to 20 per cent reversion a year. Over a lifetime, which is what this model prices, several half-lives pass and the assumption is reasonable. Over five years it is not, and a euro investor holding an unhedged world fund can and does experience real currency losses for long stretches that nothing in this model represents.

Rogoff's own point is that those half-lives are too long to be explained by sticky prices, which is the puzzle in the title. It is cited here as the measured persistence, not as an endorsement that parity holds quickly.

10. Why the answer is so often 100%

With the default risk answer, the Italian page puts all of the savings in stocks until they reach 5.6 to 8.3 years of after-tax pay, depending on age, against 1.0 to 2.7 years on the American page (Table 8). That is the model's answer and not a fault, and this section shows where it comes from. The default is 5, the value Choi, Liu and Liu's spreadsheet comes set to: an American value, not an Italian estimate, and the page says so.

How the answer is formed. The model counts two kinds of wealth: the savings typed in, and future pay and pension valued today, which Choi, Liu and Liu describe as "akin to a fixed income holding". It sets the share of the two together to hold in stocks, Merton's share, and then asks how much of the savings must be in stocks to reach it. When future pay is large and savings are small, all of the savings is not enough, and the page stops at 100%. That cap is the no-borrowing rule of the model the formula approximates, Cocco, Gomes and Maenhout's; the formula alone goes above it, and the page shows how far. As savings grow the answer falls, so the page also says, under the answer, to run it again once a year and rebalance to the new figure, using new savings first: Choi, Liu and Liu measure their rule as one followed at every age (section 4 of their paper).

The household the Italian page opens with is 45 years old, earns 30,000 euro a year after tax, has 50,000 saved and answers 5 for risk. Its future pay and pension are worth 542,085 euro, 18.1 years of pay. Merton's share of the two together is 29.3%, which is 173,364 euro of stocks against 50,000 to invest. Unconstrained, the model would hold 347% of the savings in stocks, borrowing the rest.

Three things put the Italian page there far more often than the American one.

1. The euro market. Equities beat the safe asset by a drift of 2.86 points a year in euro, against 2.12 in the United States, because the American safe rate is high, 2.96% real on 30-year TIPS, and the euro one is low, 1.54%. Take away the part of each drift that only pays for volatility and the gap is wider: the log premium Choi's discount rates read is 1.89 points in euro against 0.41. The world fund also swings less, 13.99% a year against 18.50% for the S&P 500, the long-run figure Choi uses, and Merton's share falls with the square of volatility. At risk 5 the share is 29.3% in Italy against 12.4%. The same low rates value future pay more highly through Choi's discount rates: on its own, the euro market takes human capital at 45 from 10.0 years of pay to 12.2.

2. The Italian career and pension. Italian private-sector pay keeps rising into the sixties, by 22% from 45 to 60, where the American graduate's falls by 16% (Table 6), and the pension replaces 66% of final pay rather than 40%. On its own this takes human capital at 45 from 10.0 years of pay to 14.0, and with the euro market as well, to 18.1.

3. The household each page opens with. The Italian page opens on savings of 1.7 years of pay, 50,000 euro against 30,000 of salary; the American page on 5 years, 500,000 dollars against 100,000. This changes nothing in the model, only where in Table 8 the first answer a visitor sees falls.

It is the answer the literature gives. Cocco, Gomes and Maenhout (2005) find the optimal equity share highest early in working life, because labour income stands in for safe assets, and falling towards Merton's share as savings grow. Choi, Liu and Liu's own worked example, an American graduate with risk aversion 7 and a log equity premium of 2%, holds 100% at 45 until savings reach 1.6 times permanent income, and is not down to 55% until 3.8 times (section 1.3 of their paper). The Italian page has the same shape further out because its premium is wider and its future pay larger, reasons 1 and 2, not because it does anything differently.

11. What is not validated

Three things in this variant have no external check, and none is claimed as a validated number. They are declared deviations or judgements, and they are listed here so a reader does not have to find them.

Choi's coefficients were fitted on American careers and American mortality. The career and the pension are Italian (section 7), but the discount rates that value them are Choi's, fitted on Cocco, Gomes and Maenhout's three American earnings processes and the 2019 United States life table. Pay and pension are counted as if the person is alive in each year, and the coefficients supply the chance of dying, at American rates; that cannot be made Italian from outside them. The Italian earnings process sits beside his fitted set rather than inside it, as the flat path of his own worked example does. They were also fitted for a stock market with 18.5% volatility, and the euro fund's lower volatility does not reach them at all: they read only the log premium, the compound return over the safe rate measured in logs, 1.89 points, so they value future pay as though that premium came with American risk. The page shows that figure as the log excess drift, computed as the model computes it, at the 13.99% in use. It is also just below the 2% to 4% the coefficients were fitted over, by 0.11 points. Times the premium's coefficients, 0.267 in the wage discount rate and 0.217 in the benefit rate, that is an extrapolation of 0.03 and 0.02 points of discount rate, well under a tenth of a point. Removing any of this would mean refitting the coefficients on Italian data rather than substituting inputs. The premium is Choi's own definition, the expected log return over the safe rate. His spreadsheet takes a shortcut to it, the arithmetic mean in logs less half of 18.5% squared, which is the same number when volatility is 18.5% but here would give 1.15 points and lower the reference household's answer by 1.0 point.

Leaving tax out rests on a check of one decision, not on the full life-cycle model. Section 8 holds money to one date. Simulations run for this revision on this document's snapshot, not kept in the repository, take it three steps further. Rebalancing every year leaves the effect about where it was, a little nearer none at thirty years: the taxed share is 0.74, 0.87, 0.92 and 0.95 of the untaxed one at 3, 7, 12 and 30 years, against 0.745, 0.877, 0.923 and 0.938 in Table 7. For a worker whose future pay, taken as a riskless untaxed claim, is worth one to four times their savings, tax moves the right share by about −3% to +6% at 15 to 30 years, and still about −10% at 7. For retirees who spend down, the right share is 5% to 11% below the model's answer (section 7.3). The full life-cycle model with Italian tax has not been solved. The cost of ignoring the tax, under 2 basis points a year, is computed for money held to a date three or more years away.

The level of volatility is a judgement. 13.99% is twenty-five years of one world index in euro. It is below Choi's 18.5%, which is ninety-eight years of the whole American market, and below century-long estimates for world equities, which include the 1930s: a quarter-century that holds 2008 and 2020 but no depression may be a calm sample. It is also the low end of what the same index gives over the same twenty-five years. MSCI's daily levels, checked for this revision and not kept in the repository, give about 16% from daily returns, and about 15.5% to 18.5% from monthly returns measured from any day between the 6th and the 28th of the month rather than the last, because the falls of October 2008 and March 2020 were each split across two month-end returns. Month-end is the convention, Choi's included, and it is kept. On the S&P 500 since 1928 the sampling hardly matters: daily, monthly and annual returns give 18.9%, 18.5% and 19.0% (American methodology, section 3.2). Here it does matter. For an uncapped household each point of volatility near today's figure is worth about 6 points of equity share, and less further up: the household of section 6, 45 years old with 30,000 euro of pay, 500,000 saved and risk 5, holds 61.0% at 13.99%, would hold 51.5% at 16% and 43.8% at Choi's 18.5%.

Everything else in this document is measured against something outside this project, and section 2 is the list, with these exceptions. The figure for American retirees comes from a direct solution of the full Cocco, Gomes and Maenhout model run for this project (not published).
