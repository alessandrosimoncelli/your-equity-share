Your Equity Share, Italy

What the Italian variant does differently, and what checks each number

Version 1.0, September 2026. Companion to the technical methodology, which describes the model itself. This document does not restate Merton (1969), Choi, Liu and Liu (2025), the human capital layer or the derivation of the equity share. Those are shared between the two variants and are argued there.

Every market figure here is as of 8 September 2026 and will have moved. The Python tool recomputes them from the market data it ships with; the web page runs the American variant only.

1. What this document is

One model, two countries. src/your_equity_share is shared and is not duplicated, so both variants take their dividend yield from their own index, both take real growth in earnings per share from the same hundred-year trend through Shiller, both assume no repricing, and a test asserts the growth term is identical to five decimal places rather than merely close. It runs wherever Shiller's workbook is present, which includes every Monday refresh that succeeds; the workbook is not in the repository, so a plain push skips it.

That is deliberate and it is the point of the whole arrangement. The gap between a 21.3% American answer and a 63.4% Italian one, both before tax, is the country and not the method. The Italian answer itself is after Italian tax, 58.7%, which section 8 measures; the comparison uses the figures before tax because the American variant models none. Were the model copied into two folders, the copies would be edited on different days and every later comparison would quietly mix a country difference with a code difference.

Every number below is checked against something outside this project, and section 2 is that list in one table. Where a figure cannot be checked, section 10 says so rather than dressing it up.

3. The equity sleeve

Global, not Italian. An Italian investor buying a home index would hold about 0.8% of world market capitalisation, which is Italy's weight in MSCI World. The implementation assumed is FTSE All-World through an accumulating UCITS share class, which is what most European retail investors actually hold.

The check is that this is the standard finding rather than a preference. Home bias is one of the most documented anomalies in international finance: French and Poterba (1991) measured investors holding overwhelmingly domestic portfolios against what diversification implies, and Coeurdacier and Rey (2013) survey three decades of work finding the same thing persists. No part of the literature recommends concentrating a lifetime portfolio in a market that is 0.8% of world capitalisation.

The consequence for this document is that the American variant's inputs cannot simply be reused. A different index has a different yield, a different volatility and a different currency, and each is measured below.

4. The expected return

E[Rreal] = dividend yield + real growth in earnings per share + repricing
(5)

The same three terms the American variant uses, which is what makes the two answers comparable. Today: 1.5339% + 2.2860% + 0 = 3.8198% compound, which is 4.8268% as an arithmetic mean at this volatility.

4.1 The dividend yield

Measured, from MSCI's own index levels rather than from anyone's forecast. The gross index reinvests dividends and the price index does not, so the gap between their returns over a month is that month's dividend, and twelve of those over the price at the end are a trailing yield.

Over the price at the end, and the denominator is worth a third of a point. Three constructions all sound like a trailing twelve-month yield and this year they disagree badly, because the index rose 22% across the window: dividends over the price a year ago give 1.87%, each month's dividend over that month's own starting price gives 1.70%, and dividends over today's price give 1.53%. Shiller's dividend column is a trailing annual rate divided by his last price, so the third is the object the two variants must share.

The check. AQR quote 1.6% for this index. The measurement gives 1.5339%, 0.07 points apart. Correcting the denominator improved that agreement rather than worsening it, which is the sign that the correction was right.

Gross of withholding tax, because Shiller's column is gross and the two variants have to measure one thing one way. Withholding costs 0.342 points, measured as the gap between MSCI's gross and net variants, and an American holding domestic stock pays none of it. It is recorded rather than netted off, because netting a tax into a market return hides it inside a price.

4.2 The growth term

The American variant's own hundred-year ordinary least squares trend through Shiller's real earnings per share, used unchanged as the global rate. This is the largest judgement in the variant and it has three checks, one of which is a measurement of what it costs.

AQR publish both halves, which is the only direct evidence anyone can offer: 2.7% real EPS growth for United States large cap against 2.6% for Global All Country. A tenth of a point apart.

Theory expects that. Long-run real growth in earnings per share is a return on retained capital net of share issuance, not a national characteristic, and nothing in a border makes retained euros compound differently from retained dollars over a lifetime.

And the alternative was tried and failed. tools/global_growth.py rebuilds global real dividend growth from the Jorda-Schularick-Taylor Macrohistory Database, eighteen advanced economies from 1870. The answer depends on the weighting scheme far more than on the data: weights pinned at today give 0.89% to 1.52%, pinned at the start of the window 1.86% to 3.04%, GDP shares each year 2.77% to 5.43%. An envelope 4.5 points wide against 0.25 points for the American estimator. The cause is structural: a fixed basket of countries is not an index, because an index reconstitutes and JST publishes country index returns rather than the world index's constituents.

What the substitution costs, measured. tools/us_vs_global.py puts the question to ex-US data, because comparing the S&P 500 with a world index is largely comparing the United States with itself.

The global index is about 64% American, so an American growth rate is only wrong on the other 36%, and a gap of d points costs 0.36d on the global rate.

The direction is settled and the size is not. Every estimate not built on a biased construction says the United States grew faster, so this growth term is too high, by something between a tenth and half a point. AQR's figure is a lower bound rather than a best guess, because they shrink cross-country growth differences 50% toward the global average by construction.

It is not corrected, and that is a decision rather than an oversight. Scaling by AQR's ratio looks principled and is not: they publish to one decimal place, and propagating that rounding through the 64% weight puts the implied gap anywhere between 0.10 and 0.59 points. An adjustment less reliable than the thing it adjusts is not an improvement. One consolation is real: the estimate this produces is already 0.38 points below AQR's own published figure for the same index, so even at the top of the bias range it lands under the number the firms publish.

4.3 Why not AQR's own figure

This variant took AQR's 4.20% whole until September 2026. What ended that is their own sentence about it:

We present local real (inflation-adjusted) and nominal annual compound rates of return for a horizon of 5 to 10 years. Over such intermediate horizons, starting valuations tend to be useful inputs. For multi-decade forecast horizons their impact is diluted, so theory and long-term historical averages may matter more in judging expected returns.

Both halves of their number are built to ten years, not to thirty. The payout half uses "a country-specific estimate of next-10-year real EPS growth". The earnings half multiplies the cyclically adjusted earnings yield by 1 + (g x 5) explicitly "to account for earnings growth during the 10-year window".

A current yield plus a long-run growth rate with no repricing carries no horizon at all, because nothing in it forecasts when anything happens. It is the same number at ten years or at thirty. And Table 7 of the American methodology shows it is also the one with the lowest error at thirty years, 2.25 root mean square error against 3.31 for the trailing earnings yield, which wins at ten and twenty and loses at thirty.

The mismatch was conservative, which is why it survived so long without looking like a problem. Horizon Actuarial, who survey about forty advisors and split the answers into ten-year and twenty-year horizons, report that returns "are expected to be lower in the short term compared to the long term", with differences "relatively large for equities". Vanguard, the one firm publishing both horizons off a single model run, has its thirty-year United States forecast 0.9 points above its ten-year.

AQR's two halves are entered by hand from their annual report and compared on every refresh. If either drifts more than a third of a point from ours, the refresh prints a warning; it does not stop the refresh.

4.4 Is the pairing sound

This variant pairs a global dividend yield with an American growth trend, and payout policy trades one against the other: a market that pays more of its earnings as dividends has a higher yield and correspondingly lower per-share growth. Mixing them would count the difference twice. It is the buyback double-count of the American document's section 3.1, one market over instead of one term over.

The earnings yield settles it, because E/P does not care how earnings are split between dividends, buybacks and retention. iShares, same provider and same methodology on both sides, 8 September 2026:

The concern is real and it is small, and it runs the other way. Global does pay out more of its earnings as dividends, 36.5% against 33.3%, so part of its yield advantage is policy rather than value: splitting the 0.340 point yield gap gives 0.212 to valuation and 0.128 to payout policy. But on the payout-neutral measure global is 0.638 points cheaper than the United States and this estimate credits it only 0.437, so the construction understates the global advantage by 0.20 points rather than inflating it. Each variant also lands within about a tenth of a point of its own market's earnings yield, which is what soundness looks like: equation (5) and E/P coincide when retained earnings earn the cost of equity.

5. The safe asset

The ECB's AAA euro area government curve at thirty years, deflated by the market break-even inflation rate of the longest euro linker. 1.4401%.

It took three attempts and each failed a different test, which is worth recording because each looked right at the time.

Constant maturity is the property that matters, and it is the property FRED's DFII30 has. DFII30 is not a bond: it is the thirty-year point read off the TIPS curve every day, so it describes the same horizon at every refresh. The 2046 has 19.6 years left and one fewer every year, so within a decade it would have been quoting a fifteen-year horizon under a thirty-year label.

A break-even is a price, not a forecast. It is the rate at which holding the linker and holding a nominal bond pay the same. The Survey of Professional Forecasters is an opinion, and deflating by it books an inflation risk premium as return: it sits 0.25 points above the break-even, and using it made the safe asset look better than any bond a household can actually buy. That choice was worth 5.9 points of equity share.

The construction checks against itself. The break-even is derived from the 2046, so deflating a nominal yield by it should reproduce that bond's own traded real yield of 1.4906%. It gives 1.4401%, five hundredths of a point away, and the residue is the mismatch between a thirty-year nominal leg and a 19.6-year inflation leg. The check is external: the 1.4906% is the issuer's own published figure, from the Deutsche Finanzagentur, not a number this project computed.

AAA rather than every euro area government, which yields 0.61 points more at the same maturity. That spread is compensation for a government not paying; equation (4) cannot represent default risk, and booking a credit premium as a risk-free return would lower the recommendation while looking prudent. An Italian household buying BTP is not holding this asset.

Two honest weaknesses. The curve is a composite of all AAA-rated euro area governments rather than one issuer, and its membership changes with ratings, so it is not a constant object across time. And it tracks euro area HICP rather than Italian: only BTP Italia follows Italian inflation, at five years and retail.

6. Volatility

Five years of daily euro-priced closes of the fund itself, annualised: 13.8942%. Measured on a euro-priced series, so it already carries the currency risk an unhedged global fund gives a euro investor without anything further being done to it.

It looks low against published assumptions and it is not. Horizon Actuarial's 2024 survey of 41 advisors gives 16.5% for United States large cap and 18.1% for non-US developed, both in dollars. That gap needed explaining before this figure could be used, so it was measured on one index over one window:

Two things follow and both are measurements. The currency is worth 1.73 points on the same index over the same window, because the dollar tends to rise when equities fall, so for a euro holder the currency gain offsets part of the equity loss. And the 13.8942% used here sits 0.10 points from the same index measured over twenty-five years in the same currency, which is a five-year daily estimate on one fund reproducing a twenty-five-year monthly estimate on the index.

The window barely matters in euro: 13.99%, 13.37% and 12.92% across full, twenty years and five. So the figure is not an artefact of a calm recent period.

7. The pension

66%, the Italian Treasury's projected net replacement rate for a private employee who retires in 2050 at 66 years and 2 months with 38 years of contributions: Ragioneria Generale dello Stato, Le tendenze di medio-lungo periodo del sistema pensionistico e socio-sanitario, Rapporto n. 26 (2025), Table 6.3.a, base case. Net means after tax and contributions, the same basis as the after-tax wage the page asks for.

Why that row. The model retires everyone at 67, because Choi fitted his coefficients there and the age cannot be moved from outside them, and 2050 is when today's 45-year-old retires. The same table gives 63% to 64% for its other cases near 67 and 73% for an old-age retirement at 69, so 66% sits where a retirement at 67 falls.

Why not the OECD's 79%. This variant used it until October 2026: the future net rate for an average earner in Pensions at a Glance 2025, Italy country note. The figure is right, but it describes a worker who contributes for 48 years, from 22 to the future normal retirement age of 70. Italian pensions are contributory: the pension is the contributions paid, revalued, times a conversion coefficient that rises with the age at retirement. Retiring at 67 means fewer contributions and a smaller coefficient, so paying 79% from 67 overstated the pension. Replacing it with 66% moves the Italian answer from 59.5% to 58.7%.

It is the only constant in the human capital half that can honestly be made Italian, and it is set in ITALY_CALIBRATION rather than in the market data, because it is a feature of the state rather than of the market. A larger state pension is more bond-like wealth, which leaves room for more equity in the financial portfolio, and the direction is what Bodie, Merton and Samuelson (1992) predict.

8. Tax

In Italy tax is not a household's circumstance but a fixed feature of the law that falls on exactly the two assets this model chooses between, and favours the safe one. So it is modelled rather than left to a footnote. src/your_equity_share/taxes.py holds the rates and the reasoning. The Italian answer applies it: the model reads the after-tax return and safe rate in the last row of Table 6. A comparison with the American variant, which models no tax, uses the figures before tax instead.

12.5% on interest and capital gains from Italian government bonds and those of white-list states, which includes Germany, so the safe asset here gets the low rate. 26% on everything else, including every equity fund. 0.2% a year of imposta di bollo on the value of each, charged whether or not anything was earned.

Tax falls on nominal income, so inflation is taxed. This matters more than the rate difference at low real yields: 12.5% of a 3.76% coupon is 0.47 points, taken out of a 1.44% real yield. Both sides are therefore grossed up to nominal, taxed, and deflated back, rather than having a rate applied to a real return.

Deferral pulls the other way. An accumulating fund distributes nothing, so nothing is taxed until the units are sold. Over thirty years that recovers more than half a point a year, and the safe asset gets none of it because its coupons are taxed as they arrive.

It is not the 26% that discourages equities. Levy the same 26% on both sides and the drift widens, because the safe asset is taxed every year on its whole nominal coupon while equities defer to sale. What tilts an Italian household towards bonds is the preference given to government paper, and that preference is worth 13.6 points of equity share on its own against 4.6 for the tax system as a whole.

One thing is deliberately not modelled, and it is the interesting one. A tax on investment returns normally makes a risky asset more attractive: if the state takes 26% of gains and refunds 26% of losses it has taken a 26% stake, and the household can restore the risk it wanted by holding more. That is Domar and Musgrave (1944), and under it the volatility should be scaled down by one minus the rate, which would raise the recommendation.

It does not apply here, because Italian law does not do the refund half. Gains on funds are redditi di capitale; losses on the same funds are minusvalenze, which are redditi diversi, and the two categories cannot be netted. The state takes a share of the upside and declines a share of the downside, so the volatility is left alone. A household holding individual shares would be in the other regime, where gains are redditi diversi too and losses do offset, and this model does not represent that.

9. Currency

Three of the four inputs are euro figures by construction. The dividend yield is measured on a euro-priced global index, the volatility on a euro-priced series, and the safe rate is a euro curve deflated by a euro break-even.

The growth term is the exception, and it is one term of three. It is real growth in United States earnings per share, used as the global real rate. Reading a dollar real growth rate as a euro one needs the assumption AQR state for exactly this purpose: "one simple assumption is that relative purchasing power is preserved, which implies the expected spot currency return is equal to the expected inflation differential. Under this assumption, expected real returns are the same regardless of currency."

The literature says when that is safe and when it is not, and the answer is a matter of horizon. Rogoff (1996) describes a "remarkable consensus" of three to five year half-lives for deviations from purchasing power parity, which is about 13 to 20 per cent reversion a year. Over a lifetime, which is what this model prices, several half-lives pass and the assumption is reasonable. Over five years it is not, and a euro investor holding an unhedged world fund can and does experience real currency losses for long stretches that nothing in this model represents.

Rogoff's own point is that those half-lives are too long to be explained by sticky prices, which is the puzzle in the title. It is cited here as the measured persistence, not as an endorsement that parity holds quickly.

10. What is not validated

Two things in this variant have no external check, and neither is claimed as a validated number. They are declared deviations, and they are listed here so a reader does not have to find them.

The human capital calibration is American. Cocco, Gomes and Maenhout's earnings profile, their permanent and temporary shock volatilities, and United States mortality are all fitted inside Choi's coefficients and cannot be changed from a configuration file. Only the benefit replacement rate could honestly be made Italian, and it is the Italian Treasury's projection for a retirement near 67 (section 7). This is the largest declared deviation of the variant, and removing it would mean refitting the coefficients on the Bank of Italy's Survey on Household Income and Wealth rather than substituting an input.

The tax deferral horizon is a choice. An accumulating fund is taxed once, on sale, and how much that is worth depends on when. The model fixes it at thirty years to match the maturity of the safe asset. Across ten to forty years it is worth 11.7 points of equity share, which makes it the largest free parameter in the variant. The household object already carries ages, so it can be removed rather than tuned, and docs/further-work.html records it as outstanding.

Everything else in this document is measured against something outside this project, and section 2 is the list.
