Your Equity Share, Italy

What the Italian variant does differently, and what checks each number

Version 1.1, October 2026. Companion to the technical methodology, which describes the model itself. This document does not restate Merton (1969), Choi, Liu and Liu (2025), the human capital layer or the derivation of the equity share. Those are shared between the two variants and are argued there.

Every market figure here is as of 8 September 2026 and will have moved. The Python tool and the Italian page recompute them from the market data they ship with, refreshed every Monday, except the volatility, which is fixed (section 6).

1. What this document is

One model, two countries. src/your_equity_share is shared and is not duplicated, so both variants take their dividend yield from their own index, net of withholding tax at source where a holder suffers any (MSCI's net index for the world fund, none for an American holding American stock), both take real growth in earnings per share from the same hundred-year trend through Shiller, both assume no repricing, and a test asserts the growth term is identical to five decimal places rather than merely close. It runs wherever Shiller's workbook is present, which includes every Monday refresh that succeeds; the workbook is not in the repository, so a plain push skips it.

That is deliberate and it is the point of the whole arrangement. The gap between a 20.7% American answer and a 54.4% Italian one is the country and not the method. That is the reference household used throughout: 45 years old, 100,000 a year after tax, 1,500,000 saved, risk aversion 5. Neither variant applies tax, which Choi's model does not contain, and for Italy section 8 measures why that is right and why the one tax rule in Choi's guide is not applied either. Were the model copied into two folders, the copies would be edited on different days and every later comparison would quietly mix a country difference with a code difference.

The page follows the same rule. The Italian page beside this document is not a copy of the American one: the build writes it from the same source, in Italian, with this variant's calibration and its own market file, so a fix to the page reaches both.

One difference of convention is left in and stated. The Italian yield is net of the withholding the fund suffers, and that leaves it within about 0.06 to 0.07 points a year of what the fund delivers after its own costs. The American figure is the index's own, before any fund's costs, as the premium in Choi, Liu and Liu's paper is. Section 4.1 gives the size.

The equities are assumed to be held in a broad index fund. Choi, Liu and Liu's guide says so: the model it rests on assumes a well-diversified stock market investment, such as an index fund, and the guide warns that its recommendation does not hold for a portfolio of only a few shares. The page's masthead says the same.

Every number below is checked against something outside this project, and section 2 is that list in one table. Where a figure cannot be checked, section 11 says so rather than dressing it up.

3. The equity sleeve

Global, not Italian. An Italian investor buying a home index would hold about 0.8% of world market capitalisation, which is Italy's weight in MSCI World. The implementation assumed is FTSE All-World through an accumulating UCITS share class, which is what most European retail investors actually hold.

The check is that this is the standard finding rather than a preference. Home bias is one of the most documented anomalies in international finance: French and Poterba (1991) measured investors holding overwhelmingly domestic portfolios against what diversification implies, and Coeurdacier and Rey (2013) survey three decades of work finding the same thing persists. No part of the literature recommends concentrating a lifetime portfolio in a market that is 0.8% of world capitalisation.

The consequence for this document is that the American variant's inputs cannot simply be reused. A different index has a different yield, a different volatility and a different currency, and each is measured below.

4. The expected return

1 + E[Rreal] = (1 + dividend yield)(1 + real growth in earnings per share)(1 + repricing)
(5)

The same three terms the American variant uses, combined the same way, which is what makes the two answers comparable. Today: (1 + 1.1917%)(1 + 2.2860%)(1 + 0) − 1 = 3.5049% compound, which is 4.9157% as an arithmetic mean at this volatility. The yield is net of the withholding tax the fund suffers at source; gross it is 1.5339% (section 4.1).

The terms are compounded because that is the identity for a trailing yield. With valuations constant, next year's dividend is the one just paid, D, grown by g, and the price grows at the same rate, so one plus a year's return is exactly (1 + D/P)(1 + g), where P is today's price: the Gordon growth relationship, written for the dividend already paid. Both variants compute the estimate this way. Until October 2026 they added the two terms, which left out the yield times the growth, 0.03 points here and about half a point of equity share for an uncapped household.

4.1 The dividend yield

Measured, from MSCI's own index levels rather than from anyone's forecast. The gross index reinvests dividends and the price index does not, so the gap between their returns over a month is that month's dividend, and twelve of those over the price at the end are a trailing yield.

Over the price at the end, and the denominator is worth a third of a point. Three constructions all sound like a trailing twelve-month yield and this year they disagree badly, because the index rose 22% across the window. Measured gross: dividends over the price a year ago give 1.87%, each month's dividend over that month's own starting price gives 1.70%, and dividends over today's price give 1.53%. The third is used, for two reasons. Shiller's dividend column is a trailing annual rate divided by his last price, so it is the object the two variants must share. And it is the yield the identity behind equation (5) asks for, the dividend just paid over today's price (section 4).

The check. AQR's own dividend yield for this index is 1.5% at 31 December 2025, gross. At that date the same construction gives 1.7370%, and the other two denominators 1.85% and 1.87%, so the one used is also the nearest to theirs, though 0.24 points above it. AQR say only that their figures are based on MSCI indices, so the gap is reported, not explained. Their headline yield of 1.6% is a different object: it is the average of that dividend yield and an earnings-based payout yield of 1.7%, which counts buybacks as well as dividends (their footnote 2). Until October 2026 this document compared the measurement with the 1.6%, eight months apart, and read the closeness as a confirmation.

Net of withholding tax, 1.1917%, and that is the figure used. The country a company is based in taxes the dividend it pays abroad before the money leaves, so a world fund receives its dividends already reduced. MSCI's net index reinvests them after that withholding, and over the same twelve months it takes 0.3422 points off the gross yield. No holder of the fund ever receives the money withheld: VWCE's net asset value is already net of what the fund pays, which is about a third less than MSCI deducts (next paragraph). It is a property of the fund, like its price, and not the household's own tax, which the model leaves out (section 8). An American holding American stock suffers none, which is why the American variant's gross Shiller column is the same measurement there. Until October 2026 this variant used the gross yield and recorded the withholding beside it, which credited the household with dividends the fund never receives. The page's hint for a reader's own forecast asks for the same basis: net of the withholding, so about 0.3 points below a gross forecast such as AQR's.

The net index is the right input because the fund delivers it, not because its withholding rates are the fund's. MSCI deducts withholding at the rate a non-resident institution pays without the benefit of a tax treaty, the most a fund can suffer: measured on MSCI's own indices over the same twelve months, 30.1% of American dividends and 17.4% of the rest of the world's. A fund domiciled where treaties apply pays less: VWCE, in Ireland, pays 15% on American dividends rather than 30%, and American companies paid 45.8% of the world index's dividends, so the fund suffers about 0.24 to 0.25 points of withholding where MSCI deducts 0.34, a quarter to a third less. It also pays its own costs, and the two roughly cancel. Over the five, six and seven years to August 2026, VWCE's price on Xetra rose 0.06 to 0.07 points a year less than MSCI ACWI in euro net, and about 0.58 points a year less than the gross index. VWCE tracks FTSE All-World rather than MSCI ACWI, so part of that small gap is the difference between the two indices. Measured for this revision on the fund's daily closes and MSCI's month-end levels, which are not kept in the repository.

So the Italian figure is in effect after the fund's costs, and the American one is not. The American variant uses the S&P 500's own yield, before any fund's costs, as the premium in Choi, Liu and Liu's paper is. A broad American index fund costing 0.03% a year would take the American page's opening household, 45 years old with 100,000 dollars of pay and 500,000 saved, from 37.3% to 36.8%. The difference is stated rather than removed.

4.2 The growth term

The American variant's own hundred-year ordinary least squares trend through Shiller's real earnings per share, used unchanged as the global rate. This is the largest judgement in the variant and it has three checks, one of which is a measurement of what it costs.

AQR publish a growth figure for both: 2.7% real EPS growth for United States large cap against 2.6% for Global All Country, a tenth of a point apart. It is a forecast built to narrow the differences between countries rather than a measurement of them. Each country's own growth is moved halfway to the global average, then a third of the way to forecast GDP growth, and the result is averaged with an equilibrium rate of 1.8% that is the same for every developed large-cap market (the methodology pages and Exhibit A1 of their report). So it says little about the size of the gap, and in Table 2 it is the lower end.

Theory expects the gap to be small. Long-run real growth in earnings per share is a return on retained capital net of share issuance, not a national characteristic, and nothing in a border makes retained euros compound differently from retained dollars over a lifetime.

And the alternative was tried and failed. tools/global_growth.py rebuilds global real dividend growth from the Jorda-Schularick-Taylor Macrohistory Database, eighteen advanced economies from 1870. The answer depends on the weighting scheme far more than on the data: weights pinned at today give 0.89% to 1.52%, pinned at the start of the window 1.86% to 3.04%, GDP shares each year 2.77% to 5.43%. An envelope 4.5 points wide against 0.25 points for the American estimator. The cause is structural: a fixed basket of countries is not an index, because an index reconstitutes and JST publishes country index returns rather than the world index's constituents.

What the substitution costs, measured. tools/us_vs_global.py puts the question to ex-US data, because comparing the S&P 500 with a world index is largely comparing the United States with itself.

The global index is about 64% American, so an American growth rate is only wrong on the other 36%, and a gap of d points costs 0.36d on the global rate.

The direction is settled and the size is not. Every estimate not built on a biased construction says the United States grew faster, so this growth term is too high. By how much, the two measurements in Table 2 put between 0.10 points, from AQR's pair, a lower bound because their construction narrows the gap, and 1.48 points, from MSCI's indices over 2002 to 2024, a short window in which the United States outgrew the rest by an unusual margin. This project reads the bias as a tenth to half a point, and that reading is a judgement, not a measurement. It matters: each tenth of a point of growth is worth about 1 to 3 points of equity share for an uncapped household at risk 5. The household of section 6, 45 years old with 30,000 euro of pay, 500,000 saved and risk 5, holds 51.5%; with the growth term a tenth of a point lower it would hold 49.9%, half a point lower 43.5%, and 1.48 points lower 28.2%.

It is not corrected, and that is a decision rather than an oversight. Scaling by AQR's ratio looks principled and is not: they publish to one decimal place, and propagating that rounding through the 64% weight puts the implied gap anywhere between 0 and 0.56 points, which is 0 to 0.20 points on the global rate. An adjustment less reliable than the thing it adjusts is not an improvement. One consolation is real: measured gross, as AQR measure theirs, the estimate this produces is already 0.35 points below their published figure for the same index, so the uncorrected growth term does not put it above the number the firms publish.

4.3 Why not AQR's own figure

This variant took AQR's 4.20% whole until September 2026. What ended that is their own sentence about it:

We present local real (inflation-adjusted) and nominal annual compound rates of return for a horizon of 5 to 10 years. Over such intermediate horizons, starting valuations tend to be useful inputs. For multi-decade forecast horizons their impact is diluted, so theory and long-term historical averages may matter more in judging expected returns.

Both halves of their number are built to ten years, not to thirty. The payout half uses "a country-specific estimate of next-10-year real EPS growth". The earnings half multiplies the cyclically adjusted earnings yield by 1 + (g x 5) explicitly "to account for earnings growth during the 10-year window".

A current yield compounded with a long-run growth rate, with no repricing, carries no horizon at all, because nothing in it forecasts when anything happens. It is the same number at ten years or at thirty. And Table 7 of the American methodology shows it is also the one with the lowest error at thirty years, 2.25 root mean square error against 3.31 for the trailing earnings yield, which wins at ten and twenty and loses at thirty.

The mismatch was conservative, which is why it survived so long without looking like a problem. Horizon Actuarial, who survey about forty advisors and split the answers into ten-year and twenty-year horizons, report that returns "are expected to be lower in the short term compared to the long term", with differences "relatively large for equities". Vanguard, the one firm publishing both horizons off a single model run, has its thirty-year United States forecast 0.9 points above its ten-year.

AQR's two headline halves, their combined payout yield and their combined growth, are compared with ours in section 2, from their report as of 31 December 2025.

4.4 Is the pairing sound

This variant pairs a global dividend yield with an American growth trend, and payout policy trades one against the other: a market that pays more of its earnings as dividends has a higher yield and correspondingly lower per-share growth. Mixing them would count the difference twice. It is the buyback double-count of the American document's section 3.1, one market over instead of one term over.

The earnings yield settles it, because E/P does not care how earnings are split between dividends, buybacks and retention. iShares, same provider and same methodology on both sides, 8 September 2026. The global earnings yield is taken net of the same 0.342 points of withholding as the estimate, so the two compare like with like; an American holder suffers none, so nothing comes off the American side:

The concern is real and it is small, and it runs the other way. Global does pay out more of its earnings as dividends, 36.5% against 33.3%, so part of its yield advantage is policy rather than value: splitting the 0.340 point gap between the two funds' quoted yields, 1.43% and 1.09%, gives 0.212 to valuation and 0.128 to payout policy. But on the payout-neutral measure, net of withholding, global offers 0.296 points more than the United States and this estimate credits it only 0.097, so the construction understates the global advantage by 0.20 points rather than inflating it. Before withholding the two gaps are 0.638 and 0.447, and the difference is 0.19, nearly the same, because withholding comes off both global figures: 0.342 off the earnings yield, and 0.350 off the estimate, where the yield is compounded with growth. On iShares' figures each variant also lands within 0.13 points of its own market's earnings yield, which is what soundness looks like: equation (5) and E/P coincide when retained earnings earn the cost of equity. That holds for one provider, not for every measure of earnings: the American methodology's own trailing earnings yield, from Shiller, is 3.96%, 0.56 points above the American estimate (its Table 5). So the comparison is offered within one provider and one date, which is what Table 3 is for.

5. The safe asset

The ECB's AAA euro area government curve at thirty years, read as a par yield, less the market break-even inflation rate of the longest German linker, the Bund/euro-i 2046. 1.4709%. Under the answer the page names the asset the rest of the savings goes to: inflation-linked government bonds of AAA-rated issuers, such as the Bund/euro-i and not BTP, and its Exhibit 5 gives the rate. The rate is the AAA curve's, which is why the page names AAA issuers (below).

It took three attempts and each failed a different test, which is worth recording because each looked right at the time.

Two conventions are matched before anything is subtracted. The ECB publishes its curves continuously compounded. Its technical notes say so, "The continuous method is used to compound interest rates", and give the par yield continuously compounded too. The model reads annual rates, so the thirty-year par yield of 3.6906% is first annualised, to 3.7595%. The American variant annualises DFII30 for the same reason, from the Treasury's semiannual quote. The Deutsche Finanzagentur defines its break-even as a simple yield difference between the linker and a conventional Bund, so it comes off by subtraction rather than being divided out: 3.7595% less 2.2886% is 1.4709%.

A par yield, not the spot rate. A par yield is the coupon a thirty-year bond priced at par would pay. It is the same kind of rate as DFII30, the thirty-year point of the Treasury's par real curve, and as the yields of the two bonds whose difference is the break-even. The spot rate, the zero-coupon rate this variant read until October 2026, sits above the par yield whenever the long end of the curve slopes up: on this date by 0.07 points, 3.7617% against 3.6906% continuously compounded. Read from the spot, the safe rate would be 1.5447% and the reference household's answer 1.6 points lower. Before that, the variant read the continuous rate as an annual one and divided the break-even out, which gave 1.4401% on this date.

Constant maturity is the property that matters, and it is the property FRED's DFII30 has. DFII30 is not a bond: it is the thirty-year point read off the TIPS curve every day, so it describes the same horizon at every refresh. The 2046 has 19.6 years left and one fewer every year, so within a decade it would have been quoting a fifteen-year horizon under a thirty-year label.

A break-even is a price, not a forecast. It is the rate at which holding the linker and holding a nominal bond pay the same. The Survey of Professional Forecasters is an opinion, and deflating by it puts an opinion inside a market rate. Divided out, the way a forecast of inflation is, it would give 1.6883% today, 0.22 points above the rate used and above the 2046's traded real yield, and it would lower the reference household's answer by 4.6 points. On another day the survey could sit above the break-even and the error would run the other way; the reason to refuse it is the same either way.

The construction checks against itself, and comes close. The break-even is derived from the 2046, so subtracting it from a nominal yield should land near that bond's own traded real yield of 1.4906%. It gives 1.4709%, about two hundredths of a point below. Because the break-even is a conventional Bund's yield less the 2046's real yield, what is left is the gap between the curve's thirty-year par yield and that Bund's yield, and that gap is two things: a thirty-year nominal leg against a 19.6-year inflation leg, and a composite of AAA issuers against Germany alone. A third, a zero-coupon rate against a coupon bond's yield, went with the spot rate. Nothing is fitted to close the gap. The check is external: the 1.4906% is the issuer's own published figure, from the Deutsche Finanzagentur, not a number this project computed.

AAA rather than every euro area government, whose par yield is 0.56 points higher at the same maturity. That spread is compensation for a government not paying; equation (4) cannot represent default risk, and booking a credit premium as a risk-free return would lower the recommendation while looking prudent. An Italian household buying BTP is not holding this asset.

Two honest weaknesses. The curve is a composite of all AAA-rated euro area governments rather than one issuer, and its membership changes with ratings, so it is not a constant object across time. And it tracks euro area HICP rather than Italian: only BTP Italia follows Italian inflation, at five years and retail.

How much the answer moves with it. Each tenth of a point of the safe rate, the expected return held where it is, moves the answer by about 4% of itself: 2 points on an answer near 50%, about 1 on one near 25%. The American answer moves by about 5% of itself, so the Italian one is not the more sensitive; it moves more points only because it is larger. Most of the move is Merton's: the share is proportional to the premium, and a tenth of a point on the safe rate is a tenth of a point off the premium. A direct solution of the full Cocco, Gomes and Maenhout model run for this project (not published), on this variant's calibration and the snapshot of 8 September, moves by the same amount: within 0.2 points on average at a risk aversion of 5 and 0.1 at 8, across sixteen households aged 30 to 62 with ten to thirty years of pay saved. Over September 2026 the rate rose by 0.12 points, from 1.47% to 1.59%.

6. Volatility

16.4545%, fixed. Choi, Liu and Liu's estimator, the annualised standard deviation of monthly log returns, on MSCI All Country World in euro, gross, from January 2001 to August 2026, run from every day of the month and averaged. Each of the days 1 to 28 gives one series of monthly returns, each month's level being the last one on or before that day; the figure used is the square root of the average of the 28 variances. tools/italy_volatility.py computes it from MSCI's daily index levels. Choi's 18.5% is that estimator on month-end CRSP value-weighted log returns from July 1926 to July 2024; theirs are in excess of the one-month Treasury bill and this one is on total rather than excess returns. Like theirs it is one number for a whole life, which is how they and Cocco, Gomes and Maenhout treat volatility. It is priced in euro, so it already carries the currency risk an unhedged global fund gives a euro investor without anything further being done to it. It is written into the code once and changed only by hand.

Why every day of the month. On this index over these twenty-five years the day matters. The 28 figures run from 14.32% (day 1) to 18.40% (day 23). Month-end to month-end gives 13.99%, lower than any of them, because the crashes of 2008 and 2020 each fell across two month-ends. Read from one month-end to the next, the fall of February and March 2020 is two months of −7.2% and −13.3%, with the rebound at the end of March already inside the second; read from the 23rd, it is one month of −32.2%. The fall of September and October 2008 is −8.2% and −11.2% at month-end, and −19.7% in one month from the 16th. No day is a better place to start than another, so the average is used. Over a long sample the choice hardly matters: on the S&P 500 from 1928 to 2026, month-end gives 18.5% and the average of the 28 days 18.8%, which is why Choi's figure needs no such treatment. When the figure was first fixed, in October 2026, it was the month-end one, 13.99%, on the ground that month-end is the convention; on twenty-five years it is the lowest draw the calendar offers.

Why fixed. Until October 2026 the figure was measured again every Monday from five years of the fund's daily closes, and a trailing window turns volatility into something that jumps when a crash enters or leaves it. In February and March 2025 the crash of February and March 2020 left the window: the five-year figure fell from 16.7% to 14.6% in five weeks, with no change in long-run risk. On this document's market figures that alone moves the answer for an uncapped household (45 years old, 30,000 euro of pay, 500,000 saved, risk 5) by nine points, from 50.6% to 59.7%.

Against published assumptions it sits in the middle. Horizon Actuarial's 2024 survey of 41 advisors gives 16.5% for United States large cap and 18.1% for non-US developed, both in dollars. J.P. 7. The career and the pension

The household is an Italian private-sector employee. Valuing future earnings takes three things: how pay moves with age, how much it can move by surprise, and the pension at the end. All three are Italian estimates of that one person, set in ITALY_CALIBRATION rather than in the market data because they belong to the household and the state, not to the market. With the American graduate's career and the OECD's 79%, the reference household of section 1 held 46.7%; with these, 54.4%. More bond-like wealth leaves room for more equity in the financial portfolio, the direction Bodie, Merton and Samuelson (1992) predict.

The TFR is in neither estimate, so the page asks for it. Under article 2120 of the codice civile the employer sets aside each year the year's pay divided by 13.5, which is 6.91% of gross pay once the 0.5% contribution of law 297 of 1982 comes off it, revalues it every year at 1.5% plus three quarters of inflation, and pays it when the job ends, when it is taxed separately (TUIR, articles 17 and 19). Daminato and Padula measure the wages received in each year and the Treasury's 66% is the public pension alone, so both leave it out. The page asks for it twice: the year's accrual after its tax goes in the wage, about 7.5% of net pay, and what has already accrued goes in savings, counted at about three quarters for the tax still to come. Because the wage then carries the accrual, the pension is restated on that base, 0.66 / 1.075 = 61.4% of it (section 7.2). TFR left with the employer is part of the safe share, so the percentage applies to the total and all the equity goes in the rest. This is the rule of Choi, Liu and Liu's guide for an employer's retirement contributions, which go in the wage, applied to Italy, and an employer's contribution to a pension fund goes in the wage the same way, at the nine tenths the next paragraph explains.

Pension funds count at about nine tenths, and so does TFR paid into one. Their benefits are taxed at 15%, less 0.3 points for each year of membership beyond the fifteenth, down to 9% (D.Lgs. 252/2005, article 11, paragraph 6), and only on the part not already taxed inside the fund. Counted at three quarters, as the page once said for both, a fund of 60,000 to 150,000 euro would raise the answer by up to about 3 points.

From Daminato and Padula (2024), in the Journal of the European Economic Association: the earnings process of Italian private-sector employees, estimated on the Bank of Italy's Survey on Household Income and Wealth from 1986 to 2008, for a life-cycle model of saving and portfolio choice of the same family as Choi's. They estimate it in Cocco, Gomes and Maenhout's own form, a cubic in age plus permanent and transitory shocks, which is the form this model takes. Earnings are after income tax, the definition of Jappelli and Pistaferri (2010), the same basis as the after-tax wage the page asks for.

7.2 The pension

61% of the wage the page asks for, 61.4% exactly. It is the Italian Treasury's projected net replacement rate, 66%, for a private employee who retires in 2050 at 66 years and 2 months with 38 years of contributions: Ragioneria Generale dello Stato, Le tendenze di medio-lungo periodo del sistema pensionistico e socio-sanitario, Rapporto n. 26 (2025), Table 6.3.a, base case. Net means after tax and contributions, the basis of the after-tax wage the page asks for. But the 66% is a share of the final net pay without the TFR, and the page asks for the wage with the year's TFR accrual added, about 7.5% of net pay (section 7). On that base the same pension is 0.66 / 1.075 = 61.4% of the wage the model projects, and that is the rate in ITALY_CALIBRATION. Before this revision the model applied 66% to the wage with the TFR, which paid about 71% of net pay.

Why that row. The model retires everyone at 67, because Choi fitted his coefficients there and the age cannot be moved from outside them, and 2050 is within two years of when today's 45-year-old reaches 67. The same table gives 63% to 64% for its other cases near 67 and 73% for an old-age retirement at 69, so 66% sits where a retirement at 67 falls.

Whom it understates. The same row of Table 6.3.a gives 77.0% for a retirement in 2030 and 67.4% for one in 2040, which are 71.6% and 62.7% on the page's base. The model uses 61.4% for everyone, so it understates the pension of anyone retiring before about 2040. For an uncapped household at risk 5 that understates the answer by up to about 9 points for a retirement in 2030, falling to under a point by 2040. Someone within ten years of retirement can do better than any table, and the page's pension hint says so: INPS's own estimate, "La mia pensione futura", converted to net, is more precise than 61%. It goes in the pension field with the age it starts from and the tick for the INPS old-age pension, so it replaces the imputed one. It is still an estimate that the rest of the career can move, so it is discounted as the imputed pension is, on the wage rates until work ends: at the same amount it leaves the answer where it was, and what it improves is the amount.

Whom it overstates. The model puts no ceiling on the pension. For workers first insured from 1996, or who chose the contributory method, INPS counts pay only up to 122,295 euro of gross pay in 2026 (law 335 of 1995, article 2, paragraph 18; INPS circular 6 of 30 January 2026, section 6), so for someone paid more than that the model overstates the pension, and with it the equity share. The ceiling is on gross pay, and the pension it allows after tax depends on the career and on tax, so its size here is a range rather than a figure. If the largest pension it allows is 45,000 to 55,000 euro a year after tax, it binds at 45 from a net wage of about 49,000 to 60,000 euro. For a worker aged 40 to 50 with ten years of pay saved it would lower the answer by up to about 2.5 points on 60,000 euro of net pay, 2 to 4.5 on 80,000 and 5 to 7.5 on 120,000, and by up to about 14 just below 100%. It is measured and declared rather than coded, because workers in the mixed system, insured before 1996 and mostly over about 50 today, have no ceiling, and one cap for everyone would wrongly cut their answers.

A career that outpaces average pay gets less. The 66% is for a worker whose pay grows with average pay (Table 6.3, note 1). In a contributory system a career that rises faster gets a smaller pension against its final wage, and the Treasury's own Table 6.2.a shows it: a career 0.5 points a year faster takes the 2050 gross rate from 60.3% to 55.9%. The Daminato and Padula profile rises about 1.45% a year from 29 to 66. If all of that were a career outpacing average pay, the pension would be about 0.82 of the model's, 50% of the wage rather than 61.4%, by the calculation that gives 0.913 for the Treasury's case where its table gives 0.927. The answer would then be about 1 to 2 points lower for workers aged 40 to 50 with ten years of pay saved, and up to about 4 lower just below 100%; at the flatter slope of the INPS data (section 7.1) about half that. How much of the profile is a career rather than the general rise in pay is not known, so the effect is declared and measured, not coded (section 11).

Why not the OECD's 79%. This variant used it until October 2026: the future net rate for an average earner in Pensions at a Glance 2025, Italy country note. The figure is right, but it describes a worker who contributes for 48 years, from 22 to the future normal retirement age of 70. Italian pensions are contributory: the pension is the contributions paid, revalued, times a conversion coefficient that rises with the age at retirement. Retiring at 67 means fewer contributions and a smaller coefficient, so paying 79% from 67 overstated the pension.

What the page asks for. Each adult has a pension field, a year of the pension after tax with the tredicesima, an age it starts from, left empty for a pension already paid, and a tick, "È la mia pensione INPS di vecchiaia". A pension in the field that is already being paid, or fixed by an earlier job, is riskless and is discounted at the benefit rates from today, from the year it starts. A ticked estimate of the INPS pension for a later start is not fixed yet, so it is discounted like the imputed pension it replaces, on the wage rates until work ends. Unticked, it is added to the pension the person's own work will pay and treated as fixed: a survivor's or disability pension, or one from an earlier job, whether already paid or due from a later age, or a supplementary pension already being paid. A pension fund still being built does not go here: the savings field counts it, at about nine tenths. Ticked, it replaces that pension, as the current benefit does in Choi's spreadsheet: the INPS old-age pension already drawn, an invalidity allowance or early pension that will become it, or INPS's own estimate entered with the age it starts. Before the tick, a person who still worked and already drew their own INPS pension had it counted twice, once typed and once imputed, which raised the answer by 12 to 21 points in the cases tried.

The pension the current job will pay is not typed. It is imputed at 61.4% of the wage in the last year of work, fixed then, and paid from the year after; a break typed in the middle of a career pays none of it. Wages are projected through 66, this year being the last for anyone 66 or older, and the INPS pension is added from the year after, at 61% of the last wage with the TFR. Someone of 66 or more still earning has it imputed on today's wage, added to an unticked pension and replaced by a ticked one. A partner has the same three fields.

Choi, Liu and Liu's paper has no such field. It takes Cocco, Gomes and Maenhout's measure of income, which counts the pensions and transfers a household receives while it works as labour income (their footnote 5), so such a pension rides on the wage process there and is discounted at the wage rate. Here it is riskless, the way Bodie, Merton and Samuelson (1992) value a riskless income. For a pension of 10,000 euro paid while working, the riskless reading gives about 4 to 8 points more equity than the wage-rate one for a 45-year-old on 30,000 euro with fifteen to thirty years of pay saved, more nearer 100%, and 1 to 4 points at 60. On the American calibration, a direct solution of the full Cocco, Gomes and Maenhout model run for this project (not published) finds the riskless reading right in direction for every household tried and about a quarter short in size, and the wage-rate reading further off (American methodology, section 7.6). Typed only in the year-by-year box instead, with the field empty, it is read the same way, because the page takes a pension in the box's first year as one already being paid; only a pension that first appears in a later year of the box is discounted at the wage rate through the last year of work.

Every year is entered as Choi, Liu and Liu's guide asks, "assuming you are still alive in each future year": the calculations already allow for the chance of dying, at United States rates (section 11). The year-by-year box follows the age, wage and pension fields until a year in it is edited. From then on the pension amount, its starting age and its tick are locked, and they go on telling the model which part of each typed benefit is fixed, so a pension entered there before the edit keeps its treatment. A note under the box says that the typed years replace the wage and pension above until Reset to projection. A reset brings the projection back and unlocks the fields.

7.3 Retirees

A retired household's answer carries four known errors, and a fifth the page no longer invites. They point different ways and partly offset, and none is corrected.

Near 100%: too much equity, and with more saved, too little. A direct solution of the full Cocco, Gomes and Maenhout model run for this project (not published), on this document's snapshot, for a retiree with a pension of 20,000 euro, finds the formula too high where its answer comes down from 100%: at risk 5 by 3 to 9 points, with 6 to 7 years of pension saved at 67 and 4 to 5 at 85, and at risk 8 by up to 11 points with 2 to 4 years. With more saved it runs low, by about 2 to 5 points with 12 to 20 years of pension at risk 5. In the benchmark, as in Cocco, Gomes and Maenhout's model and in the tool's discount rates, the retiree lives on a riskless pension and dies at American rates. Where the corner falls moves with risk aversion and with the market, so no fixed amount of savings marks it. The Italian page prints no warning about it, and the American page has dropped the one it had: measured there together with the next error, which pulls the other way, it left no direction safe to print (American methodology, section 8.6).

The thirty-year rate: too little equity. The riskless asset that matches a retiree's spending is a ladder of inflation-linked bonds running to about 100. With spending that falls with age, as it does in Cocco, Gomes and Maenhout's model, its duration is about 14 years at 67, 11 at 75 and 7 at 85, and the euro real curve sits lower there than at thirty years. On this document's date the construction of section 5, with the break-even read between the 2033 and 2046 linkers, gives 1.17% at ten years against 1.4709% at thirty, and the Finanzagentur's 2033 linker yields 0.99% real against 1.49% for the 2046. At the rate of each age's ladder, the retired households with a pension that were tried would hold about 2 to 4 points more equity between 67 and 75, about 4.5 more at 80 and 6 at 85. The American methodology (section 8.6) weights each year by the chance of being alive in it instead. On the euro curve that gives about 11 years at 67, 8 at 75 and 5 at 85, and about 4 to 7 points more equity between 67 and 75, 6.5 at 80 and 8.5 at 85. The two weightings bracket the effect.

The two together: mostly too little. Solved at the rate of each age's ladder rather than at thirty years, the same direct solution of the full model leaves the formula within about 3 points of it where the answer comes down from 100% at 67 to 75, and below it everywhere else at risk 5: by up to 7 to 9 points at 67 to 75 and up to 11 at 85, and by up to 15 with the ladder weighted by survival. At risk 8 the formula is up to 5 points high near 100% and up to 11 points low with more saved.

Couples: too much equity. The model counts both pensions for as long as either spouse lives, and Choi's spreadsheet sums the two adults the same way. Under Italian rules a surviving spouse keeps their own pension and 60% of the other's, the reversibilità, and less when their own income is high. With that rule at the full 60%, a retired couple's pension wealth is about 8 to 10% lower than the model counts in the couples tried, aged 65 to 80 with pensions of 12,000 to 24,000 euro each, more for older couples, about 12% for two people of 80, and lower still where the survivor's own income cuts the 60%. So the part of the answer above the no-pension share, 24.7% at risk 5, is too high by about that proportion: 1.2 to 4.7 points in the couples tried, the most where savings are small against the pensions.

Tax while spending down: too much equity. Section 8 holds the money to one date. A retiree sells some of both funds every year, so part of the money is taxed after only a few years, where tax lowers the right share most. A simulation run for this revision on this document's snapshot, and not kept in the repository, of retirees aged 67 to 75 who spend their savings over 14 to 20 years and rebalance every year puts the right share 5% to 11% below the model's answer: about 2.5 to 4.5 points too much equity.

Money set aside: no longer invited. The page used to describe its money as having "no planned use or withdrawal date", which invited a retiree to keep years of spending out of the savings typed in. That money still sits somewhere, usually in cash, so the household ended up with less equity than the model wants for its whole wealth. The page now speaks of money meant to last the rest of your life, with no fixed date to spend it, and its savings hint tells a retiree to include what they will draw down over the years and leave out only what they will take from savings this year beyond their pension, and money for a dated goal. That is Choi, Liu and Liu's accounting. The wealth their rule applies to is what the household holds plus this year's income, less this year's spending: cash on hand less consumption, equation 5 and section 1.3 of their paper. That equals what the household holds whenever this year's income pays for this year's spending, and the drawing down in later years is the model's own consumption.

8. Tax

The Italian answer reads the market figures as they are, and this section says why. Choi, Liu and Liu do not model tax: their variables are "implicitly after-tax where relevant". The guide to their spreadsheet has one tax rule, to cut the safe rate by the marginal tax rate when bonds are held in a taxable account. For an American saving in a 401(k) or an IRA the market figures are close to after-tax ones and the rule does not apply. For Italy the rule is not applied, because it does not describe Italian law. The law taxes both funds, not only the bonds, and at sale rather than every year: 26% on the gain of an equity fund, 12.5% on a fund of government bonds of Italy and white-list states, in proportion to what it holds in them (Agenzia delle Entrate, Circolare 19/E of 2014, section 5), and 0.2% a year of imposta di bollo on the value of each. Both rates fall on the nominal gain and only when units are sold, death counts as a sale (Circolare 19/E of 2013, section 2.4), and a loss on one fund cannot be set against a gain on another.

The guide's rule would move the answer the wrong way. Cutting only the safe rate, by the 12.5% a government bond fund pays, takes it from 1.4709% to 1.2870% and raises the answer. For an uncapped household aged 45 to 65 at risk 5 it adds up to about 7 or 8 points just above the savings where the answer leaves 100%, and less as savings grow, about 2 points at fifty times pay (4.1 for the reference household), and slightly more for younger households. Taxing the equity fund as well, which is what the law does, lowers it, as the rest of this section measures.

Tax takes a share of the risk as well as of the return. A fund held for many years is usually in gain when it is sold, 81% of the time at seven years and 97% at thirty on this document's snapshot (Table 7), so the state takes about a quarter of the gain whether markets did well or badly: it shares the swings, not only the average. That is the effect Domar and Musgrave (1944) described. Taking tax off the expected return and leaving the volatility as it is counts the first half and misses the second, and this variant did exactly that until October 2026.

Measured directly. tools/tax_check.py takes the two funds through the law as written: the equity fund's price is uncertain, 26% of its nominal gain is paid on sale with no credit for a loss, 12.5% of the bond fund's, and the stamp duty every year. It then finds the equity share a household with risk aversion 5 should hold until a given date, with the tax and without it, exactly rather than by simulation.

It does share only the upside, but after three years a fund is below cost when sold more than one time in four, after seven about one time in five, and after twelve about one time in eight, so the upside is most of what there is for money held long.

9. Currency

Three of the four inputs are euro figures by construction. The dividend yield is measured on a euro-priced global index, the volatility on a euro-priced series, and the safe rate is a euro curve less a euro break-even.

The growth term is the exception, and it is one term of three. It is real growth in United States earnings per share, used as the global real rate. Reading a dollar real growth rate as a euro one needs the assumption AQR state for exactly this purpose: "one simple assumption is that relative purchasing power is preserved, which implies the expected spot currency return is equal to the expected inflation differential. [...] Under this assumption, expected real returns are the same regardless of currency."

The literature says when that is safe and when it is not, and the answer is a matter of horizon. Rogoff (1996) describes a "remarkable consensus" of three to five year half-lives for deviations from purchasing power parity, which is about 13 to 20 per cent reversion a year. Over a lifetime, which is what this model prices, several half-lives pass and the assumption is reasonable. Over five years it is not, and a euro investor holding an unhedged world fund can and does experience real currency losses for long stretches that nothing in this model represents.

Rogoff's own point is that those half-lives are too long to be explained by sticky prices, which is the puzzle in the title. It is cited here as the measured persistence, not as an endorsement that parity holds quickly.

10. Why the answer is so often 100%

With the default risk answer, the Italian page puts all of the savings in stocks until they reach 4.2 to 6.7 years of after-tax pay, depending on age, against 1.0 to 2.7 years on the American page (Table 8). That is the model's answer and not a fault, and this section shows where it comes from. The default is 5, the value Choi, Liu and Liu's spreadsheet comes set to: an American value, not an Italian estimate.

How the answer is formed. The model counts two kinds of wealth: the savings typed in, and future pay and pension valued today, which Choi, Liu and Liu describe as "akin to a fixed income holding". It sets the share of the two together to hold in stocks, Merton's share, and then asks how much of the savings must be in stocks to reach it. When future pay is large and savings are small, all of the savings is not enough, and the page stops at 100%. That cap is the no-borrowing rule of the model the formula approximates, Cocco, Gomes and Maenhout's; the formula alone goes above it, and the page shows how far. As savings grow the answer falls, so the page also says, under the answer, to run it again once a year and rebalance to the new figure, using new savings first: Choi, Liu and Liu measure their rule as one followed at every age (section 4 of their paper).

The household the Italian page opens with is 45 years old, earns 30,000 euro a year after tax, has 50,000 saved and answers 5 for risk. Its future pay and pension are worth 543,263 euro, 18.1 years of pay. Merton's share of the two together is 24.7%, which is 146,306 euro of stocks against 50,000 to invest. Unconstrained, the model would hold 293% of the savings in stocks, borrowing the rest.

For one household, four fifths of the extra equity is the market and one fifth is the career and pension. Between the two opening households the smaller savings count for about as much as the market, and the cap hides part of every effect: any two of the three changes take the American opening household to 100%, so whichever comes third shows nothing.

It is the answer the literature gives. Cocco, Gomes and Maenhout (2005) find the optimal equity share highest early in working life, because labour income stands in for safe assets, and falling towards Merton's share as savings grow. Choi, Liu and Liu's own worked example, an American graduate with risk aversion 7 and a log equity premium of 2%, holds 100% at 45 until savings reach 1.6 times permanent income, and is not down to 55% until 3.8 times (section 1.3 of their paper). Its premium is about the Italian one, 1.98%. The Italian page has the same shape further out because at risk 5 and 16.45% volatility Merton's share is larger, 24.7% against 15.5% in the example, and its future pay is worth more, reasons 1 and 2, not because it does anything differently.

11. What is not validated

Four things in this variant have no external check, and none is claimed as a validated number. They are declared deviations or judgements, and they are listed here so a reader does not have to find them.

Choi's coefficients were fitted on American careers and American mortality. The career and the pension are Italian (section 7), but the discount rates that value them are Choi's, fitted on Cocco, Gomes and Maenhout's three American earnings processes and the 2019 United States life table. Pay and pension are counted as if the person is alive in each year, and the coefficients supply the chance of dying, at American rates; that cannot be made Italian from outside them. The Italian earnings process sits beside his fitted set rather than inside it, as the flat path of his own worked example does; for Italian workers a direct solution of the full Cocco, Gomes and Maenhout model run for this project (not published) puts the formula within about 5 points of it except just below 100% (section 7.1). They were also fitted for a stock market with 18.5% volatility, and the euro fund's lower volatility, 16.45%, does not reach them at all: they read only the log premium, the compound return over the safe rate measured in logs, 1.98 points, so they value future pay as though that premium came with American risk. That figure is the log excess drift, computed as the model computes it, at the 16.45% in use. It is just below the 2% to 4% the coefficients were fitted over, by 0.02 points, while the log safe rate, 1.46%, is inside the 0% to 2% they were fitted over. Times the premium's coefficients, 0.267 in the wage discount rate and 0.217 in the benefit rate, the shortfall is an extrapolation of under half a hundredth of a point of discount rate. Removing any of this would mean refitting the coefficients on Italian data rather than substituting inputs. The premium is Choi's own definition, the expected log return over the safe rate. His spreadsheet takes a shortcut to it, the arithmetic mean in logs less half of 18.5% squared, which is the same number when volatility is 18.5% but here would give 1.63 points and lower the reference household's answer by 0.4 points.

Leaving tax out rests on a check of one decision, not on the full life-cycle model. Section 8 holds money to one date. Simulations run for this revision on this document's snapshot, not kept in the repository, take it three steps further. Rebalancing every year leaves the effect about where it was, about a hundredth stronger: the taxed share is 0.74, 0.87, 0.93 and 0.95 of the untaxed one at 3, 7, 12 and 30 years, against 0.752, 0.887, 0.936 and 0.955 in Table 7. For a worker whose future pay, taken as a riskless untaxed claim, is worth one to four times their savings, tax moves the right share by about −2% to +4% at 15 to 30 years, and still about −9% at 7. For retirees who spend down, the right share is 5% to 11% below the model's answer (section 7.3). The full life-cycle model with Italian tax has not been solved. The cost of ignoring the tax, under 2 basis points a year, is computed for money held to a date three or more years away.

The level of volatility is a judgement. 16.45% is Choi's estimator, taken from every day of the month, on twenty-five years of one world index in euro. Choi's 18.5% is ninety-eight years of the whole American market, and the window matters: over the same twenty-five years American stocks in dollars give 17.03% by the same estimator (Table 5), so a quarter-century that holds 2008 and 2020 but no depression may be a calm sample. Scaling Choi's 18.5% by the ratio of the world index in euro to American stocks in dollars over this window would give 17.87%, or 16.88% on month-end returns. Month-end alone gives 13.99%, and section 6 says why it is not used. Twelve-month returns, overlapping, on the month-end series give 16.62%. On the S&P 500 since 1928 none of these choices matters much: month-end and the average over the days of the month give 18.5% and 18.8%, and daily, monthly and annual returns 18.9%, 18.5% and 19.0% (American methodology, section 3.2). Here they do. For an uncapped household each point of volatility near the figure used is worth about 3.7 points of equity share: the household of section 6, 45 years old with 30,000 euro of pay, 500,000 saved and risk 5, holds 51.5% at 16.45%, and would hold 63.2% at month-end's 13.99%, 50.9% at 16.62%, 46.8% at 17.87% and 45.1% at Choi's 18.5%.

The pension's career slope is declared, not corrected. The 61.4% is the Treasury's rate for a career that keeps pace with average pay, restated on the wage with the TFR. If the whole rise of Daminato and Padula's profile were a career outpacing average pay, the contributory pension would be about 0.82 of it, and the answer about 1 to 2 points lower for workers aged 40 to 50 with ten years of pay saved, up to about 4 just below 100% (section 7.2). How much of the profile is a career and how much is the general rise in pay is not known.
